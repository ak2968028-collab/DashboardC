#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Many accounting methods make wood harvests look carbon neutral by offsetting them with forest growth that would happen anyway. The authors built a carbon harvest model (CHARM) that tracks carbon after each harvest against unharvested forest and discounts the flows at 4% for 40 years. Wood harvests from 2010 to 2050 cost 3.5–4.2 Gt CO₂e per year, close to emissions from agricultural expansion. Harvests rise 54% by 2050. The result is robust to the discount rate, and cutting harvests is an underappreciated climate option.


## 1. Background

After agriculture, wood harvest has most reduced carbon in vegetation and soils. Life-cycle studies treat 'sustainable' wood as carbon neutral, national reports net all changes in managed forests (3 billion ha, three-quarters of the world's forests), and land-use papers report harvests net of regrowth. Gross- emission estimates ignore regrowth (pp. 110–111).


## 2. Aim of the study

To estimate the present and future carbon costs of global wood harvests from 2010 to 2050 under different supply scenarios, using time discounting (p. 110).


## 3. What the authors did

A country fixed-effects model projected demand for four product classes from population, GDP and time. Demand was traced back to roundwood harvest (3,586 million m³ in 2010, 52% wood fuel). CHARM compared carbon in all pools after harvest with an unharvested forest and discounted emissions and removals at 4% for 40 years. Seven supply scenarios were tested (Table 1), and discount rates of 0–6% over 40 or 100 years (pp. 111–113).


## 4. Main findings

Harvests rise 54%, from 3.7 to 5.7 billion m³ (long-lived products +69%, paper +128%, wood fuel +22%, industrial waste fuel +91%). Annual carbon costs are  3.5–4.2 Gt CO₂e across scenarios; 2010 harvest levels alone cost 3.2 Gt CO₂e/yr, and existing demand gives 78% of costs. ## 5. Conclusions

1. Ongoing and rising wood harvests have major carbon costs that common accounting hides. 2. Forest growth that would occur anyway should not be credited to new harvests. 3. Reducing harvests is a rarely identified mitigation 'wedge' that can buy time, if it does not shift emissions elsewhere (p. 114).


## 6. Limitations

- Soil carbon losses and indirect effects (such as road building) are omitted, so results are probably conservative.
- Wood fuel demand projections are the most uncertain.
- No economic counterfactual of how markets would respond; global, not local.
