#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Mycorrhizal fungi trade soil nutrients for plant carbon. Pooling 194 datasets, the authors give the first global estimate of how much plant carbon goes to their underground mycelium: about 3.58 Gt C (13.12 Gt CO₂e) per year, equal to 36% of fossil fuel CO₂ emissions in 2021. Ectomycorrhizal fungi take the largest share of host NPP (13.1%), arbuscular fungi 6.2%. Crops give much less (3.3% on average; under 0.1% for wheat, barley and maize). How long this carbon stays in soil is not yet known.


## 1. Background

Plants and mycorrhizal fungi have formed partnerships for more than 400 million years. Up to 80% of plant phosphorus and 20% of nitrogen can come via the fungi. About 75% of land carbon is stored below ground, and the fungi sit at a key entry point of carbon into soil food webs, but their global role in moving carbon into soil was under-explored (p. R560).


## 2. Aim of the study

To give the first global quantitative estimate of carbon allocated from plants to mycorrhizal mycelium, and to review how these fungi add carbon to, and lose it from, soils.


## 3. What the authors did

They collected 194 datasets (mostly isotope labelling, plus mass balance, FACE and modelling) from forests, shrublands, heathlands, grasslands and croplands in Central Europe, the USA, South America and Asia; none were found from Southeast Asia, Oceania or Africa. External mycelium was used as a common proxy; where only hyphal-compartment labelling was available, values were cut by 7% to remove exudates (pp. R563–R564). Mean allocations by fungal type were applied to global NPP, masked by maps of mycorrhizal vegetation and plant functional groups (p. R565).

### 4.2 Crops and plant types

Crops allocated 3.3% ± 0.55 of NPP to AM fungi (N = 55), four times less than wild grasses and forbs. Medic and ryegrass gave about 5%, while  wheat, barley and maize gave less than 0.1%, possibly because of fine roots, breeding for fertiliser response and intensive cultivation. Herbaceous plants gave 6.5% and woody plants 2.3% (N = 6 only) (p. R565).

### 4.4 Mechanisms

- Living hyphae draw carbon into soil and help create and stabilise soil aggregates.
- Dead fungal tissue (necromass) forms a scaffold that protects organic matter; melanin slows decay.
- Exudates feed microbes and can form stable mineral-associated organic matter.
- Losses: fungal respiration is 6–14% (AM) and 6–25% (EcM) of soil respiration; fungi can also increase priming (pp. R566–R568).

## 5. Conclusions

1. Mycorrhizal fungi are a major global carbon pool, drawing 3–13% (up to 50%) of host NPP below ground. 2. About 13 Gt CO₂e a year moves through plants into mycorrhizal mycelium; this may be an over- or underestimate. 3. Mycorrhizal fungi should be included in global climate and carbon-cycle models and in conservation policy and practice (p. R569).


## 6. Limitations

- Data mostly from single time points; few studies for ErM, OM and DSE fungi.
- No data from Southeast Asia, Oceania or Africa; tropical forests, savannas and the global south are under-represented (pp. R564, R568).
- Only external hyphae were counted, so the flux may be underestimated; losses through respiration were not assessed.
- How long the carbon stays in soil is unknown, so the flux is not the same as long-term storage (p. R569).
