#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Renewable power has become much cheaper: solar PV electricity fell from $0.417 to $0.048 per kWh between 2010 and 2021 (−88%), and onshore wind fell 68%. Climate change may cut wind and hydropower output by up to about 40% in some regions, while solar is least affected. Hydropower is the most environmentally damaging renewable; wind and biomass have the smallest impacts. Renewables could decarbonise 90% of the power sector by 2050.


## 1. Background

Fossil-fuel energy causes more than  75% of global greenhouse gas emissions and about  90% of CO₂ emissions (p. 741). Energy demand is expected to rise 56% by 2040. Renewables are needed to decarbonise energy, but rising temperatures, extreme winds, sea-level rise and lower rainfall can affect their output, and their environmental effects are less studied (p. 742).


## 2. Aim of the study

To  review  the  costs  of  renewable  energy,  the  impact  of  climate  change  on  renewables,  the environmental and economic impacts of renewables, and decarbonisation in different countries, focusing on solar, wind, biomass, hydropower and geothermal energy.


## 3. What the authors did

A literature review. Cost data come mainly from IRENA (2022); the comparison of environmental effects is taken from Rahman et al. (2022); country studies are summarised in Table 4. No new data were collected.

### 4.2 Climate impacts on renewables

- Wind: small overall changes in some regions, but up to −40% in northern Canada and Alaska under a high-emission scenario (p. 750).
- Hydropower: up to −41% generation by 2050 in Portugal; South America is the most vulnerable region (pp. 750–752).
- Solar: the least affected source; PV output may rise in some places and fall about 4% over much of Africa (p. 751).
- Biomass: climate can alter productivity, growth, chemical composition and soil microbes; +1 °C reduces global wheat yields by 6.0%, maize by 7.4% and rice by 3.2% (p. 751). 4.3 Environmental impacts Hydroelectric plants are the most damaging (greenhouse gases, eutrophication, sediment, drying rivers, floods, soil erosion, resettlement). Solar PV must be installed carefully. Wind turbines and biomass plants have negligible impact and are recommended for wide use. Most country studies since 2016 show renewables reduce life-cycle CO₂, but a study in a remote block of Uttaranchal, India (Akella et al. 2009) found a rise in emissions (pp. 753–756). 4.4 Economic impacts Fossil fuels were subsidised by $5.9 trillion in 2020. About $4 trillion per year of renewable investment is needed by 2030 for net zero, but avoided pollution and climate impacts could save up to $4.2 trillion per year by 2030, and the energy transformation could add $98 trillion to global GDP in 2016–2050 (pp. 756– 757).

### 4.5 Countries

China supplies about two-thirds of the world's solar panels and nearly half of its wind turbines; India uses auctions, private energy trading and procurement tracking; Japan sets yearly renewable obligations for utilities; Nigeria uses distributed generation and smart grids; South Africa uses green funds and carbon credits (pp. 757–759).


## 5. Conclusions

1. Except hydropower and geothermal, renewable costs have fallen sharply since 2010. 2. Climate change can cut wind and hydro output by up to about 40% regionally; solar is the most resilient. 3. Hydropower has the largest environmental impact; wind and biomass the smallest. 4. Renewables could supply 65% of world electricity by 2030 and decarbonise 90% of the power sector by 2050, helping limit warming to 1.5 °C.


## 6. Limitations

- Global review of studies of mixed quality; costs and impacts vary by location.
- Little detail on small rural or off-grid systems.
- Some numbers in the paper are garbled (for example, jobs written in dollars on p. 757), so check the primary sources.
- Often cited as 2022 (online date); the issue year is 2023.
