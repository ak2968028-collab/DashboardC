#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

About 120.7 Mha of India's land is degraded, most of it by water and wind erosion. The review compiles agroforestry systems developed by Indian research for eroded, sloping, ravine, mined, waterlogged, saline, sodic and acid lands. Reported carbon sequestration ranges from 1.55 to 20–25 Mg C/ha/yr depending on zone and system (Middle Gangetic Plains: 2.32), and hedgerows cut soil loss by 94% in north-east India. The authors call for agroforestry business models and policy support.


## 1. Background

India has about 120.7 Mha of degraded land and wasteland (NAAS 2010). Water erosion affects 73.3 Mha and wind erosion 12.4 Mha of arable land, together about 71% of the degraded area; chemical degradation (salinity, sodicity, acidity) and physical degradation (waterlogging, mining) make up the rest. A newer estimate (SAC 2021) gives 97.8 Mha (29.7% of the country). Drivers include 2.07 Mha of tree cover loss in 2001–2021, uncontrolled grazing (5.65 Mha), shifting cultivation (7.6 Mha) and heavy fertiliser use (pp. 1–2).

## 2. Aim of the study

To review the achievements of Indian agroforestry research in rehabilitating degraded land, summarise the systems suited to each kind of degradation and agro-climatic zone, and set out future strategies.


## 3. What the authors did

This is a literature review. The authors compiled degraded-area statistics, the drivers of degradation, the prevalent agroforestry systems of each agro-climatic zone with their carbon sequestration and food production potential (Table 4), and systems tested on sloping, gully and shifting-cultivation land, mined and waterlogged land, and salt-affected and acid soils (Tables 5–9). Values are quoted from the cited studies.

### 4.2 Eroded, sloping and ravine land

Hedgerow cropping on sloping land in north-east India reduced soil loss by 94% and runoff by 78% (Saha et al. 2012). Grass filter strips and Indigofera hedgerows conserved SOC (43%) and available N, P and K. In the Himalayan foothills, bamboo with semicircular trenches stopped runoff and soil loss after the fifth year. In ravines, Acacia nilotica and A. tortilis with Cenchrus produced 28.7 and 27 Mg/ha of fuelwood. A silvipasture at Jhansi (Uttar Pradesh) lost only 0.9 Mg/ha of soil, against 12–43 Mg/ha on deforested black soil.


### 4.3 Salt-affected and other problem soils

Chemical degradation covers about 17.4 Mha. For sodic soils, Acacia nilotica, Albizia procera, Leucaena, neem and Eucalyptus hybrid were the best-suited trees; Prosopis juliflora with Kallar grass can be grown for 4–5 years before the land returns to crops. Strip plantations of Eucalyptus tereticornis lowered a high water table by 0.75 m in five years and raised crop yields 3–4 times on waterlogged saline soil (Table 9).


## 5. Conclusions

1. Agroforestry (agri-silviculture, silvipasture, agri-horticulture) has strong potential for sustainable rehabilitation of degraded land, with ecological and economic benefits. 2. India's many suitable species should be brought into agroforestry-based business models; supply chains, buy-back with MSP, insurance, credit and quality planting material are needed. 3. Agroforestry can help meet the Bonn Challenge goal of restoring 26 Mha of degraded land by 2030 and the land degradation neutrality target.


## 6. Limitations

- A review: all numbers come from cited studies of mixed quality and methods.
- Table 4 gives one system and one study per zone; values are not zone averages and depend on species, density and tree age.
- Few values are specific to eastern Uttar Pradesh or the Varuna basin.
