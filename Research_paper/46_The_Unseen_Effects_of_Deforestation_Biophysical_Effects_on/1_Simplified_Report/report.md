#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Forest policy values only carbon, but forests also change climate through albedo, evapotranspiration and canopy roughness. The authors combined modelled biophysical effects of deforestation with a new latitude-by-latitude estimate of the CO₂ in aboveground biomass. Tropical deforestation causes strong net global warming from both routes. Up to 50°N, deforestation still warms the planet overall; beyond 50°N, brighter snowy land makes it cool. Locally, at all latitudes, biophysical effects far outweigh CO₂: forests cut heat extremes. Carbon-only metrics undervalue forests.


## 1. Background

Forests hold over 360 Pg C in vegetation and over 800 Pg C with soils, and land ecosystems remove 29% of annual CO₂ emissions. Forests also affect climate directly through albedo, evapotranspiration and roughness, which moderate local temperature (p. 2).


## 2. Aim of the study

To review biophysical mechanisms, synthesise their forcing across latitudes, and combine them with a new analysis of the CO₂ effect of forest biomass to assess the net local and global effect of deforestation (p. 1).


## 3. What the authors did

Local effects were synthesised from satellite, flux-tower and in situ studies. Global biophysical effects per 10° band came from the Davin and de Noblet-Ducoudré (2010) total-deforestation experiment (land only), with BVOC effects scaled from Scott et al. (2018). The CO₂ effect used a 2016 global 500 m aboveground carbon map, 2.12 Gt C per ppm, 23% ocean uptake, and a sensitivity of 0.107 °C per 10 ppm (pp. 4–6).


## 4. Main findings


### 4.1 Local temperature

Tropical forests are cooler than nearby cleared land by 0.2–2.4 °C (mean 0.96 °C); temperate effects are 0.02–1.0 °C (mean 0.4 °C); boreal clearing usually cools by under 1 °C a year. Warming from clearing is larger by day and in the hot season. Past deforestation explains about one third of the increase in hottest-day intensity (pp. 6–7).

## 5. Conclusions

1. Carbon-centric metrics do not capture the value of forests for mitigation and adaptation. 2. In the tropics, biophysical effects amplify carbon benefits; reforestation there cuts warming about three times more per area than in boreal zones. 3. Forests provide local climate stability at all latitudes, so policy should treat mitigation and adaptation together (pp. 8, 10).


## 6. Limitations

- Global biophysical estimates come from one model of complete, idealised deforestation.
- Aboveground carbon only; synthesis of studies with different methods.
- Future warming, drier conditions, less snow and disturbance will change these effects.
