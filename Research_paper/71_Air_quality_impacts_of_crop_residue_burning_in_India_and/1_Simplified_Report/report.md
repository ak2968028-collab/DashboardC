#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Using the GEOS-Chem adjoint model for 2003–2019, the authors estimate that crop residue burning in India caused 44,000–98,000 premature deaths a year from PM₂.₅, costing about 23 billion USD a year. Punjab, Haryana and Uttar Pradesh account for 67–90% of the deaths, and six Punjab districts alone for 40%. Fire-related exposure came 64% from Punjab, 11% from Haryana and 5.7% from Uttar Pradesh. Burning two hours earlier in November in Punjab could avert about 9,600 deaths a year on average.


## 1. Background

PM₂.₅ exposure causes more than four million premature deaths a year worldwide, 10–25% of them in India. India produces about 500 Mt of crop residue a year and burns about 100 Mt, mainly after the wheat harvest (April–May) and the rice harvest (October–November) in north-west India. Bans and fines have had limited effect.


## 2. Aim of the study

To estimate how agricultural burning affects air quality and health across India, and to quantify the benefit of targeted district-level and timing-based actions.


## 3. What the authors did

GFED v4.1s burning emissions were combined with district crop data. The GEOS-Chem adjoint model computed how the PM₂.₅ exposure of India's population responds to burning at each location and hour from 2003 to 2019. An Integrated Exposure Response function gave premature deaths, and an India- adjusted value of statistical life gave monetised cost. Scenarios tested 1% emission cuts by district and shifts in burning time.

- Post-monsoon (rice) burning gave 64% and pre-monsoon (wheat) burning 28% of the added exposure.
- Punjab's impact was six times Haryana's, partly because Haryana grows more basmati rice, whose straw is used.
- Sangrur, Patiala and Ludhiana provide two-thirds of the benefit achievable in Punjab.
- The cost rose from 29% to 40% of India's total health expenditure between 2003 and 2019.

## 5. Conclusions

1. Residue burning imposes a large and growing health cost on India. 2. A small number of districts and burning times could be prioritised for the greatest benefit. 3. Targeted, potentially low-cost actions can complement mechanisation, pending cost-effectiveness and feasibility studies.


## 6. Limitations

- Covers primary PM₂.₅ only; no ozone and no greenhouse gases.
- The IER function was not developed for India and treats all PM₂.₅ as equally toxic.
- A single assumed daily burning profile; the timing benefit needs field checks.
- No village- or Varanasi-level results.
