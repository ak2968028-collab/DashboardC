#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Fossil fuels still supplied 61.3% of the world's electricity in 2020. This review proposes a roadmap to a low-carbon grid built on three technological changes: saving energy on the demand side, generating more efficiently, and substituting fossil fuels with renewables and low-carbon nuclear. It argues that energy options must be judged on five dimensions (technical, environmental, economic, social and political-institutional) and that smart grids, storage, finance and policy are needed to make renewable electricity reliable and affordable.


## 1. Background

The energy sector produces about two-thirds of global CO₂, and electricity generation about 26% of total greenhouse gas emissions (pp. 2, 5). Global primary energy use grew from 155 EJ in 1965 to 557 EJ in 2020, and fossil fuels supply about 85% of it. In 2020, coal, gas and oil produced  61.3% of global electricity (p. 11). The Stockholm, Rio and Johannesburg conferences linked energy to the environment, the economy and society, making sustainable energy development a key part of sustainable development. The Paris Agreement aims to keep warming well below 2 °C and pursue 1.5 °C.


## 2. Aim of the study

To review energy transition strategies and propose a roadmap for sustainable, low-carbon grid electricity generation, transmission, distribution and use, in line with the Paris Agreement, that nations can adapt to their local conditions.


## 3. What the authors did

A literature review using critical discourse analysis. The authors cover greenhouse gases from power generation, global electricity generation, sustainable energy development, five dimensions of energy sustainability, and strategies for the transition (policy, planning, renewables, decentralised and smart grids, research and development, and Earth radiation management). They summarise energy options (Table 5) and strategies (Table 6). No new data were collected.

### 4.2 Three technological changes

Transition  strategies  consist  of  energy  savings  on  the  demand  side,  generation  efficiency  at  the production level, and substitution of fossil fuels by renewables and low-carbon nuclear. Large-scale renewables should go with efficiency gains in existing non-renewable plants, which still help reduce and stabilise costs.


### 4.3 Five dimensions of energy sustainability

Sustainable development has economic, social and environmental pillars; energy sustainability also needs technical and political-institutional dimensions (Table 4, p. 36). Environmental indicators include life-cycle GHG emissions in kg CO₂-eq per kWh; economic ones include generation cost, payback and affordability; social ones include acceptance and access.


### 4.4 Grid technologies

- About 10% of grid power is lost in transmission and distribution, 40% of it on the distribution side (p. 32).
- Smart grids allow two-way communication, consumers to sell power, variable pricing and peak shaving.
- Microgrids balance variable renewables with conventional units; storage and resilient grids absorb solar and wind.
- In 2019 about 37% of global electricity came from low-carbon sources; transport is about 14% of GHG emissions, so electrifying it matters (p. 33).
- IEA: efficiency could deliver over 40% of the targeted cut in energy-related GHG emissions between 2020 and 2040 (pp. 33–34). 4.5 Barriers and finance Renewables are competitive, especially for remote areas, but low- and middle-income people lack capital. Financing is needed for  solar PV, micro-hydro, wind pumps, biodigesters and biogas and improved woodstoves; higher initial investment is a key barrier (p. 31). 4.6 Technology routes Three groups of technology are needed: conventional mitigation, negative emission technologies that capture and store carbon, and technologies that change the Earth's radiative energy budget (solar radiation management).

## 5. Conclusions

1. Sustainable development cannot be achieved without sustainable energy. 2. Both renewable and cleaner non-renewable sources have a role in the short- and long-term transition. 3. A resilient grid with storage, smart grids and microgrids is needed for variable renewables. 4. Electrification of transport, industry and homes with renewable power, plus waste-to-energy, will cut emissions. 5. The transition needs supportive policy, finance, institutions and decision-support models.


## 6. Limitations

- A broad global review of grid electricity, not rural off-grid systems; no new data.
- Most numbers are secondary (IEA, BP, UN); some are inconsistent within the paper (for example the 2018 fuel shares on p. 3, and older GWP values in Table 1), so cite the primary sources.
- No village or Indian case data.
