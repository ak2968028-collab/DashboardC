#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

From 537 publications and more than 5,000 paired measurements, the authors found that in grasslands organic fertiliser raised aboveground biomass by 56% (inorganic 42%) and SOC by 19% (inorganic 2%), without the 18% loss of species richness seen with chemical fertiliser. In croplands (SOC only), organic inputs also raised SOC: by 32% in cooler areas (more than grasslands) and 34% in warmer areas (less than grasslands' 62%). Tillage may explain the weaker warm-climate cropland response.


## 1. Background

Inorganic fertiliser increases plant biomass but often reduces plant diversity in grasslands, a well-known trade-off (pp. 1–2). Organic fertilisers such as manure and compost may be a nature-based alternative. Grasslands cover about 41% of the land surface (excluding Greenland and Antarctica) and store about 34% of terrestrial carbon (p. 2). It was unclear whether croplands and grasslands differ in SOC response at comparable organic rates (p. 2).


## 2. Aim of the study

To compare organic and inorganic fertilisation effects on aboveground biomass, plant diversity and SOC, and to find which climate and soil factors change these effects. One hypothesis was that croplands would show SOC gains comparable to grasslands (p. 2).


## 3. What the authors did

The search yielded 537 publications (described as 537 experiments in the abstract) on all continents except Antarctica (pp. 1, 7).

## 4. Main findings


### 4.1 Grasslands

- Inorganic fertiliser raised biomass by 42% but cut species richness by 18% and evenness by 6% (p. 2).
- Organic fertiliser raised biomass by 56% with no diversity loss; compared with inorganic it gave 10% more richness and evenness (p. 2).
- Organic fertiliser raised SOC by 19% vs unfertilised and 15% vs inorganic; inorganic raised SOC by 2% (p. 2).

- At comparable organic rates, SOC increases were equal in grasslands and croplands (p. 3).
- The SOC gain rose with organic rate and fell as initial SOC rose, more strongly in croplands (p. 3).
- With warming, the SOC response rose in grasslands but fell in croplands; the authors suggest tillage in warm regions speeds SOC decay (pp. 3, 5).

## 5. Conclusions

Organic fertilisation is a potential nature-based solution that raises forage production and soil carbon in grasslands without the diversity loss caused by inorganic fertiliser. Locally sourced, non-contaminated manure or industrial organic fertiliser should be used to avoid exotic species and other side effects (pp. 1, 5).


## 6. Limitations

- Biomass and diversity results are for grasslands only; croplands were analysed for SOC only (p. 7).
- Grassland trials were mostly short (under 10 years) (p. 7).
- SOC is expressed as percent change, not as stock per hectare.
- GHG emissions from manure (N₂O, CH₄) and crop yields were not assessed.
- Few experiments come from South Asian croplands (thesis author's assessment). CORRECTION TO THE EARLIER NOTES The 56%, 42%, 19%, 15% and 2% figures apply to grasslands. Croplands are not simply 'weaker': they gained more SOC than grasslands in cooler climates and less only in warmer ones.
