#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

This qualitative review explains how biochar, especially combined with compost, manure or bio-organic fertilisers, improves the rhizosphere: better structure, water retention, CEC and pH, more enzyme activity, and less nutrient leaching and runoff. Its stable carbon can last decades to centuries. The review gives no effect sizes, notes that gains are larger in sandy than clay soils, and lists cost, inconsistent quality and logistics as the main barriers.


## 1. Background

Biochar is the carbon-rich product of pyrolysis of crop residues, forestry by-products or municipal waste, typically at 300–700 °C with little oxygen (p. 2). Temperatures above 500 °C give more stable carbon and larger surface area; below 400 °C the biochar keeps more nutrients (p. 8). Heavy use of chemical fertiliser has caused soil degradation, water pollution through runoff, loss of soil biodiversity and GHG emissions from production and transport (p. 2). Organic fertilisers can replace part of it, but how biochar interacts with the rhizosphere (the soil around roots) and soil enzymes in this setting had not been explored enough (p. 3).


## 2. Aim of the study

To compile knowledge on the short- and long-term effects of biochar, especially with organic fertilisers, on rhizosphere properties, soil enzyme activity and nutrient dynamics, and to identify challenges and gaps. The review starts from the hypothesis that combining biochar with organic fertilisers significantly improves rhizosphere properties and enzyme activity (p. 3).


## 3. What the authors did

The authors reviewed published studies on rhizosphere properties, organic fertilisers and their limits, biochar's properties and mechanisms, enzyme activity, short- and long-term effects, environmental implications (GHG, runoff, ISFM) and practical barriers. Two summary tables cover soil properties (Table 1, p. 16) and plant growth (Table 2, p. 19). No new data or meta-analysis was produced.

### 4.2 Nutrients, enzymes and time

- Organic fertilisers release nutrients slowly and vary in composition, which can delay supply and reduce yield in intensive systems (p. 7).
- With biochar and manure, dehydrogenase, phosphatase and protease activity rise in the short term; N retained in biochar pores supports urease and other N-cycle enzymes (pp. 10, 13).
- Short term: better aeration, water retention and less leaching, particularly in sandy or highly permeable soils (p. 13).
- Long term: stable carbon can remain for decades or centuries; more SOM and stable aggregates reduce erosion (p. 14).
- Biochar holds N, P and K and releases them gradually, reducing leaching and volatilisation and the frequency of fertiliser applications (p. 14). 4.3 Environment and practice
- Biochar adsorbs ammonium, potassium, calcium, nitrate and phosphate, keeping them out of rivers and lakes and reducing eutrophication (p. 17).
- The review states biochar reduces N₂O and CH₄ (pp. 15, 17) but gives no effect sizes and lists GHG effects as needing further investigation (p. 19).
- Biochar is described as a cornerstone of integrated soil fertility management (ISFM) (p. 18).
- Barriers: cost for smallholders, no quality standard, limited production capacity, technical skill needs, possible heavy metals, and pH effects of over-application (p. 18).

## 5. Conclusions

Combining biochar with organic fertilisers can raise productivity, reduce environmental impacts and support climate mitigation. Research should focus on long-term effects across seasons, crop-specific

responses in maize, wheat and rice, site-specific recommendations by soil type, use with synthetic fertilisers, and the best feedstock, pyrolysis temperature and dose (pp. 1, 19–20).


## 6. Limitations

- Qualitative review with no effect sizes for yield, SOC or GHG.
- Benefits depend on soil, feedstock and dose; clay soils may gain less than sandy soils (p. 19).
- Most cited evidence is short-term (p. 19).
- The GWP values quoted (N₂O ~300, CH₄ ~25) are older values; use the GWPs of the IPCC report the thesis follows.
