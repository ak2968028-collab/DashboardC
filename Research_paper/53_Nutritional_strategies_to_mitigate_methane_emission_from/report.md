#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

This review gathers feeding strategies to cut enteric methane, the main livestock GHG in India (10.09 Mt CH₄, 73.3% of agricultural methane; INCCA 2010). Tender grasses, more concentrate and balanced rations reduce methane; ration balancing cut it by about 10–11% in Indian field trials and is the most practical large-scale option. Fats, oils, nitrate, probiotics and many herbal additives (Harit Dhara up to 17–20%) also work, but antibiotics and ionophores are not recommended, and herbal products need formulation and commercialisation.


## 1. Background

India has the world's largest cattle and buffalo population. Methane has 27–28 times the warming potential of CO₂. Buffaloes (50 kg CH₄/animal/yr) made up 42% of India's livestock methane in 2003, and methanogenesis wastes about 7% of gross energy intake (pp. 157–158).


## 2. Aim of the study

To review laboratory and field-tested nutritional strategies for mitigating livestock methane in India, to guide future research (p. 157).


## 3. What the authors did

A narrative review grouped by approach: nutrient supplementation, greens and legumes, fats and fatty acids, microbial interventions, plant bioactive compounds and essential oils. Table 1 lists 22 plants with anti-methane metabolites (pp. 159–161).

That is implausible (larger than half of global GHG emissions) and should not be cited.


## 5. Conclusions

1. Ruminants are the main methane producers; farmers adopt measures only if they pay. 2. Ration balancing, green fodder and fewer high-producing animals are practical levers. 3. Herbal products need dosing, formulation and commercialisation, with government support for industry (pp. 157, 162–163).


## 6. Limitations

- Narrative review; study quality and methods vary.
- Many results are in vitro or from non-Indian animals and diets.
- Costs and adoption barriers are not analysed in detail.
- Some figures need checking against the original sources.
