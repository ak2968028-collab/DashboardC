#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

The authors trace India's fertiliser policies from the 1950s and their effects. Subsidies made urea very cheap (farmers pay 22% of the world price), and food grain output kept pace with a tripling population, with gains linked to lower rural poverty. But unequal subsidies caused urea overuse and P and K underuse, lowering efficiency and raising N₂O and nitrate losses; soil N surplus rose to 100.8 kg N/ha by


## 2021. They recommend recalibrating subsidies, soil-test-based advice and gradual urea price

liberalisation while keeping P and K affordable for smallholders.


## 1. Background

India is self-sufficient in food grains, but production is resource-intensive, cereal-centric and regionally imbalanced. From 1971–72 to 2021–22 food grains rose 3× while N use rose 11.3×, P₂O₅ 14.2× and K₂O 5.71× (p. 1).


## 2. Aim of the study

To  analyse,  with  quantitative  data,  how  fertiliser  policies  shaped  consumption  and  nutrient management, and their effects on rural producers, the environment and climate (p. 1).


## 3. What the authors did

They combined policy history (FAI, Katyal 2023) with consumption, production and poverty data (FAI, Government of India) and FAOSTAT soil N budgets and Tier 1 N₂O, using growth rates, regressions and an interrupted time series of policy effects adapted from Praveen et al. (2020) (pp. 2–3, 5).

CORRECTION TO THE OLD NOTES The old notes said India's N balance became surplus only after 1984. That holds only when mineral fertiliser is the sole N input (Katyal 2023). With all N inputs counted, India already had a surplus of 17.5 kg N/ha in 1961, rising to 100.8 kg N/ha in 2021 (p. 8, Fig. 5).


## 5. Conclusions

1. Fertiliser policies were crucial for food security and socio-economic progress. 2. Unequal subsidies caused imbalanced use, lower efficiency, N₂O emissions and nitrate pollution. 3. Recalibrate subsidies, promote soil-based recommendations, and liberalise urea gradually while keeping P and K affordable (pp. 10–11).


## 6. Limitations

- National secondary data may miss regional variation (the authors say so).
- N₂O from FAOSTAT Tier 1; policy effects from trends and time series, not causal designs.
- Reform may meet political resistance; organic inputs get little attention (p. 10).
