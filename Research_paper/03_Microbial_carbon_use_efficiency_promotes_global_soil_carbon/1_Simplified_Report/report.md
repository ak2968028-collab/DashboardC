#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Using 132 field CUE–SOC pairs and 57,267 soil profiles fed into a microbial model with data assimilation and deep learning, the authors show that microbial carbon use efficiency (CUE) is positively linked to soil organic carbon and is at least four times as important as plant inputs, decomposition or vertical transport in setting global SOC storage. A 2% rise in CUE would raise global SOC by 10% (136 PgC).


## 1. Background

Soils store more carbon than other terrestrial ecosystems. Losses of soil organic carbon (SOC) could accelerate warming, whereas storing CO₂ in soils can help mitigate it. The classical view holds that SOC storage depends mainly on how much plant carbon enters the soil and how fast it decomposes, yet studies of these controls have not explained SOC storage well (p. 981). KEY TERM Microbial carbon use efficiency (CUE) is the ratio of microbial growth to carbon uptake: the share of carbon used in metabolism that goes to growth rather than respiration. It is an integrative measure of whether microbes build or lose soil carbon (pp. 981–982).

Whether CUE is positively or negatively linked to SOC was debated. High CUE could build SOC through microbial by-products and necromass, or it could speed SOC loss by raising enzyme production and priming (Fig. 1, p. 982).


## 2. Aim of the study

To test how CUE relates to SOC storage across the globe, to rank it against six other controls (plant carbon inputs, input allocation, non-microbial carbon transfer, substrate decomposability, environmental modifiers and vertical transport), and to find which environmental variables shape CUE (pp. 981–984).


## 3. What the authors did

- Meta-analysis: 132 pairs of measured CUE and SOC at 46 locations from 16 isotope-labelling studies, analysed with linear mixed-effects models (p. 982; Methods).
- PRODA: a microbial-explicit soil carbon model was fitted to each of 57,267 vertical SOC profiles by data assimilation, assuming steady state, and a deep-learning neural network then generalised the parameters to the globe (p. 982).
- Sensitivity analyses: (1) removing the spatial variation of one component at a time; (2) finding the change in each component needed to raise global SOC by 10% (p. 984).
- Permutation analysis of environmental variables to explain spatial CUE patterns (p. 984; Methods).

## 4. Main findings


### 4.1 CUE and SOC are positively linked

In the meta-analysis, CUE was positively correlated with SOC after accounting for method differences: log₁₀(SOC) = 1.16 + 0.76 × CUE, explaining 54.7% of the variation (Fig. 2a). High CUE went with both more microbial and more non-microbial carbon. Measured CUE averaged 0.33 with both carbon and oxygen isotope methods (Methods). The CUE retrieved from the 57,267 global profiles also correlated positively with SOC, although the link weakens with depth (pp. 982–983).

### 4.3 Global patterns and what controls CUE

CUE and SOC share similar patterns, with lower values in tropical than in boreal regions. Tropical plants supply much more carbon, but low CUE and high decomposition prevent high SOC stocks. In boreal regions decomposition rates are high, but cold, permafrost and waterlogging restrict loss; vertical transport is strongest in permafrost regions (pp. 983–984).  Soil structural properties (such as bulk density, texture and porosity) explained more of the spatial variation in CUE than geographic, climatic, soil chemical or vegetation variables (p. 984).


## 5. Conclusions

1. Microbial CUE plays a pivotal role in determining SOC storage at the global scale. 2. A high CUE promotes SOC storage more than loss: more carbon goes to biomass and by-products, whereas low CUE sends more carbon to respiration. 3. Future research should prioritise microbial processes, alongside decomposition and carbon inputs, to predict SOC feedbacks to climate and to enhance SOC sequestration (pp. 984–985).


## 6. Limitations

- The meta-analysis lacks data from tropical and arid regions and from clay soils (Methods).
- A steady-state soil carbon cycle is assumed at each profile; projecting future change needs time- varying parameters and microbial acclimation and adaptation (pp. 982, 984).
- How sensitive the ranking of CUE is to different model structures still needs testing (p. 984).
- Our assessment: CUE requires isotope incubations, so a village study can use it as a concept and measure soil structure and SOC instead; the paper does not test farm management practices.
