#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Across 84 grasslands on six continents, plant diversity (Shannon index) was positively related to soil carbon content and to the soil C:N ratio. The links were weak across all sites (R² = 0.09) but strong in the warmest, driest and most arid quartiles (R² 0.38–0.56), and absent in cooler, moister sites. Plant biomass was related to SOC but not to diversity, so diversity seems to act through organic matter quality, not quantity. Restoring plant diversity likely enhances SOC sequestration, especially in warm and arid climates.


## 1. Background

Plant diversity is positively related to SOC in many ecosystems, usually explained by higher productivity and so more carbon input (p. 1). Most evidence comes from short, small-scale experiments at single sites, which may underestimate slow soil effects, and little is known about how climate changes the relationship (pp. 1–2).


## 2. Aim of the study

To test how plant diversity relates to SOC and soil C:N ratio across grasslands spanning wide climate gradients, and whether the link works through plant biomass (quantity) or organic matter quality (p. 2).


## 3. What the authors did

The authors used pre-treatment data from 84 natural and semi-natural Nutrient Network grassland sites (19 grassland types), collected 2007–2020 with one protocol (pp. 2, 7). Each site had about 30 plots of 5 × 5 m; plant cover was recorded in a 1 × 1 m subplot to compute the Shannon index, aboveground biomass was clipped (74 sites), and three soil cores from 0–10 cm were pooled per plot and analysed in one laboratory (p. 7). Regressions used site means, across all sites and within quartiles of temperature, precipitation and aridity (21 sites each), and structural equation models compared pathways (p. 8). CORRECTION TO THE EARLIER NOTES The earlier notes said diversity was measured as species richness and the Shannon index. All analyses in the paper use the Shannon index only (pp. 7–8).


## 4. Main findings

- SOC was positively correlated with the Shannon index across all sites (R² = 0.09, P = 0.005) and rose by a factor of 2.6 from one species to a Shannon index of 2.5 (p. 3).
- Soil C:N ratio was also positively correlated with diversity (R² = 0.09, P = 0.006) (p. 3).
- The diversity–SOC link was significant only in the warmest (MAT > 15.58 °C, R² = 0.38), driest (MAP < 523 mm, R² = 0.46) and most arid (AI < 0.50, R² = 0.42) quartiles (p. 5, Table 2).
- Diversity was not correlated with plant biomass (P = 0.119), while biomass was correlated with SOC (R² = 0.09, P = 0.008) (pp. 2, 6).
- A model with diversity acting through C:N ratio fitted better than one acting through biomass (p. 7, Fig. 1).
- Across sites, SOC fell with temperature (R² = 0.19) and rose with the aridity index (R² = 0.38); clay content had no effect (P = 0.864) (p. 6).

## 5. Conclusions

Plant  diversity  affects  SOC  through  organic  matter  quality,  in  a  climate-dependent  way,  so  the biodiversity–ecosystem relationship depends on climate (p. 7). Because grasslands hold about one third of global terrestrial carbon, diversity loss could jeopardise SOC storage, particularly in warm and arid climates, and management that restores diversity likely enhances SOC sequestration (pp. 1, 7).


## 6. Limitations

- Observational: shows correlations; the authors note soil organic matter may also influence plant diversity (p. 7).
- Only the top 10 cm was sampled, and SOC is a content (g/kg), not a stock (p. 7).
- Grasslands only; no Indian sites named in the main text.
- One sampling per site, so no change over time is measured.
