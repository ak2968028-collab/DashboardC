#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

In 2023 fossil CO₂ emissions reached 10.1 ± 0.5 GtC (+1.3%) and land-use change added 1.0 ± 0.7 GtC, a total of 11.1 ± 0.9 GtC (40.6 GtCO₂). Of this, 5.9 GtC stayed in the atmosphere, the ocean took 2.9 GtC and the land only 2.3 GtC, its lowest since 2015 because of El Niño and Canadian wildfires. Fossil emissions were projected to rise a further 0.8% in 2024, with CO₂ reaching 422.45 ppm, 52% above pre- industrial. India's fossil emissions grew fastest among the big emitters (+3.6% per year over 2014–2023), but its share of fossil CO₂ since 1850 is 3%. Only 65 GtC remain for a 50% chance of 1.5 °C, about 6 years at 2024 emissions.


## 1. Background

Knowing how much CO₂ humans emit and where it goes (atmosphere, ocean, land) is needed to understand the carbon cycle, support climate policy and project future climate change (p. 968). The Global Carbon Budget is the Global Carbon Project's annual update. The 2024 edition gives final data for 2023, a projection for 2024, cumulative emissions since 1750 and 1850, and an updated remaining carbon budget (p. 969).


## 2. Aim of the study

To describe and synthesise the data sets and methods that quantify the five major components of the global carbon budget and their uncertainties, and to document changes in methods and data since the previous edition (p. 968).


## 3. What the authors did

Fossil CO₂ emissions (EFOS) come from energy statistics and cement production data; land-use change emissions  (ELUC,  mainly  deforestation)  from  land-use  data  and  four  bookkeeping  models;  the atmospheric growth rate (GATM) from direct CO₂ measurements; the ocean sink (SOCEAN) from ocean biogeochemistry models and fCO₂-based data products; and the land sink (SLAND) from dynamic global vegetation  models.  Atmospheric  inversions,  atmospheric  oxygen  measurements  and  Earth  system models give extra lines of evidence on the land and ocean sinks. The budget imbalance BIM = EFOS + ELUC − (GATM + SOCEAN + SLAND) measures imperfect data and understanding. Uncertainties are ±1σ (p. 968). Units: 1 ppm = 2.124 GtC and 1 GtC = 3.664 GtCO₂ (p. 970).


## 4. Main findings

- In 2023, EFOS was 10.1 ± 0.5 GtC per year (10.3 ± 0.5 without the cement carbonation sink), +1.3% on 2022; ELUC was 1.0 ± 0.7, for a total of 11.1 ± 0.9 GtC per year (40.6 ± 3.2 GtCO₂) (p. 968).
- In 2023, GATM was 5.9 ± 0.2 GtC per year (2.79 ± 0.1 ppm), SOCEAN 2.9 ± 0.4 and SLAND 2.3 ± 1.0, with a budget imbalance of −0.02 GtC per year; CO₂ averaged 419.31 ± 0.1 ppm (p. 968).
- The 2023 land sink was 1.6 GtC lower than in 2022 and the lowest since 2015, driven by tropical ecosystems at the onset of El Niño and large Canadian wildfires (p. 969).
- Preliminary 2024: fossil CO₂ +0.8% (−0.2% to 1.7%) to 10.2 GtC; total 11.4 GtC (41.6 GtCO₂); CO₂ 422.45 ppm, 52% above about 278 ppm in 1750 (pp. 968–969).
- Over 2014–2023, fossil CO₂ grew 0.6% per year; India +3.6%, China +1.9%, EU27 −2.1%, USA −1.2% per year (p. 985; Table 9, p. 1012). Total emissions (fossil plus land use) showed zero growth over the decade (p. 969).
- Cumulative fossil CO₂ 1850–2023: USA 120 GtC (24%), EU 80 GtC (16%), China 75 GtC (15%), India 15 GtC (3%) (p. 985).
- Land use: 1.1 ± 0.7 GtC per year over 2014–2023; deforestation about 1.7 GtC per year, of which re-/afforestation and regrowth offset 1.2 GtC (p. 969).
- Remaining carbon budget from January 2025 (50% likelihood): 65, 160 and 305 GtC (235, 585, 1110 GtCO₂) for 1.5, 1.7 and 2 °C, about 6, 14 and 27 years at 2024 emissions (p. 1011).

## 5. Conclusions

The mean and trend of the budget components are consistently estimated over 1959–2023 with a near- zero overall imbalance, although discrepancies of up to about 1 GtC per year remain for annual to semi- decadal variability (p. 968). Total emissions have plateaued over the last decade but are not falling (p. 969). The 1.5 °C budget is about six years of current emissions, and even net zero by 2040 would emit 90 GtC, more than the 65 GtC left, unless emissions turn net negative afterwards (p. 1013).


## 6. Limitations

- Global and national data only; no district or village values.
- CO₂ only; CH₄ and N₂O are covered by papers 2 and 11.
- 2024 figures are projections and every edition revises earlier years, so use the latest edition when citing numbers.
- Land-use change emissions remain the most uncertain flux; values are in GtC (× 3.664 for GtCO₂). CORRECTION TO THE EARLIER NOTES The earlier notes listed the authors as 'Friedlingstein, P., O'Sullivan, M., Jones, M. W., Andrew, R. M., Bakker, D. C. E., …'. Bakker is not an author of this edition; the first ten are Friedlingstein, O'Sullivan, Jones, Andrew, Hauck, Landschützer, Le Quéré, Li, Luijkx and Olsen (121 authors; vol. 17, pp. 965–1039). The earlier notes also called the 1850–2023 country shares (India 3%, USA 24%) shares of 'historical emissions'; they are shares of fossil CO₂ only, without land-use change (p. 985). 'Largest uncertainty: land-use change emissions' was widened to the paper's three named gaps. All other old numbers were confirmed. INCONSISTENCIES IN THE PAPER The abstract gives the 2023 rise in fossil CO₂ as 1.3% (p. 968), while Sect. 3.1.3 gives 1.4% (p. 985); both describe a rise of 0.14 GtC to 10.1 GtC. This summary uses the abstract value and flags the difference.
