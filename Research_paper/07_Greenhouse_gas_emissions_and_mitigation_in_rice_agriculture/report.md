#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Rice paddies give about 48% of cropland GHG emissions. Field data average 283 kg CH₄ and 1.7 kg N₂O per ha per year, or 7,870 kg CO₂e/ha/yr (94% from CH₄) and 0.9 kg CO₂e per kg grain. Organic matter and continuous flooding drive CH₄; the N rate drives N₂O. Warming may raise CH₄ by 15–23%. New varieties, non-continuous flooding and straw removal cut GHG by 24%, 44% and 46% on average, but the best mix depends on the local seasonal CH₄ pattern (AM pp. 2–3, 15).


## 1. Background

Rice is the staple for more than half of the world's people and provides 20% of dietary energy. Paddies cover about 1.7 million km², and production was about 757 million tonnes in 2020, led by China, India, Bangladesh, Indonesia and Vietnam. Global consumption rose from 157 million tonnes in 1960 to 520 million tonnes in 2022 (AM p. 2). Rice gives about 48% of cropland GHG emissions, 22% of agricultural CH₄ and about 11% of agricultural N₂O. Current paddy emissions are estimated at 24–31 Tg CH₄/yr and 130 Gg N₂O/yr (AM p. 2). HOW PADDIES EMIT In flooded, oxygen-free soil, methanogens produce CH₄ from organic matter; up to 90% of it escapes through the rice plant. N₂O forms during nitrification and denitrification and peaks when soils are alternately wet and dry (Box 1).


## 2. Aim of the study

To review how much CH₄ and N₂O rice paddies emit, what drives the emissions, how elevated CO₂ and warming will change them, and which practices cut emissions while keeping yields (AM p. 3).


## 3. What the authors did

The authors compiled in-situ field measurements worldwide (Supplementary Data) to estimate mean emissions per hectare and per kg of grain, grouped seasonal CH₄ patterns at several hundred sites, and reviewed experiments and meta-analyses on elevated CO₂, warming and management: cultivar, water, organic matter, mineral N, tillage and crop establishment, liming and new techniques. Gases were combined with 100-year GWP (AM pp. 3–4).


## 4. Main findings


### 4.1 Emission levels

Table A. Mean paddy emissions from field data (AM p. 3) Measure Value CH₄ 283 kg/ha/yr N₂O 1.7 kg/ha/yr Area-scaled GHG 7,870 kg CO₂e/ha/yr (~94% CH₄) Yield-scaled GHG 0.9 kg CO₂e/kg grain CH₄ at 10°S–10°N 614 kg/ha/yr CH₄ above 40°N 272 kg/ha/yr Emissions vary up to two orders of magnitude, even within a country. China, India and Indonesia emit 22–38%, 11–19% and 7–9% of global rice CH₄, and 27%, 17% and 15% of rice N₂O (AM p. 3).


### 4.2 Drivers

- Organic matter: straw raises CH₄ by 92% and manure by 60%; organic additions raise area-scaled GHG by 66–85% (AM p. 4).
- Water: non-continuous flooding (NCF: mid-season drainage, intermittent irrigation, AWD) cuts CH₄ by 53% in the rice season; multiple drainages could cut 4.1 Tg CH₄/yr globally (AM p. 4).
- Nitrogen: N fertiliser raises N₂O by 182%; the direct N₂O-N emission factor is 0.52% (0.15–1.3%), close to IPCC's 0.3% (flooded) and 0.5% (NCF) (AM p. 4).
- Seasonal patterns: early peak (~40% of sites), two peaks (~27%), late peak (~24%; 43% of Indian sites) and bell-shaped (~8%) (AM pp. 4–5). 4.3 Trends and climate change Yield-scaled CH₄ fell 38–55% from the 1980s to the 2010s, mainly through higher yields and more drainage (AM pp. 6, 15). Elevated CO₂ raises CH₄ by 4–40% (the global effect may be only 3.7% given straw use) and N₂O by 27%; warming raises CH₄ by 15–23% and N₂O by 26%; together they raised CH₄ by 71% and N₂O by 36% (AM pp. 2, 6–8).

### 4.4 Mitigation

- Drainage raises N₂O by 105% on average but usually not enough to cancel the CH₄ gain; 33 kg N₂O/ha/season occurred when fields dried at high soil N (AM pp. 9–10).
- NCF could suit 76% of rice area without yield loss, but uptake is low in parts of South and Southeast Asia where monsoon rain makes drainage hard (AM p. 10).
- Straw addition raised CH₄ with a GWP 3.2–3.9 times its SOC gain, so organic additions generally give no net climate benefit (AM p. 10).

## 5. Conclusions

1. Optimising organic matter, water and nitrogen management is the most promising way to cut paddy GHG while keeping yields. 2. The best mix depends on the local seasonal CH₄ pattern. 3. More field data are needed in low latitudes such as the Philippines, Indonesia and southern India, and models such as DNDC need more processes (AM pp. 15–16).


## 6. Limitations

- Global means hide up-to-100-fold local variation; low-latitude values rest on few observations (AM p. 3).
- Most climate-change experiments are short (under 5 years) (AM p. 16).
- Fallow-season emissions and nursery emissions are poorly measured (AM pp. 5, 13).
