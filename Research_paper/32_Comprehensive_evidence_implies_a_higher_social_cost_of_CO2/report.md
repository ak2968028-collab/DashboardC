#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Using the new open-source GIVE model, the authors estimate a mean social cost of CO₂ of $185 per tonne ($44–$413, 5–95% range, 2020 US$) at a 2% near-term discount rate, 3.6 times the US government's $51. The lower discount rate explains most of the increase, and updated damage functions most of the rest; heat-related mortality ($90) and agriculture ($84) dominate. Because several damage types are still missing, the estimate is probably conservative.


## 1. Background

The social cost of CO₂ (SC-CO₂) is the money value of damages to society from one extra metric tonne of CO₂. In regulatory analysis, the climate benefit of a policy is usually the change in CO₂ emissions multiplied by the SC-CO₂. The US government has used it in more than 60 major regulations, and agencies in Germany, Canada and Mexico also use it (p. 687). A 2017 report of the US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM) found that existing estimates no longer reflected the latest research and recommended improvements in scientific basis, transparency and treatment of uncertainty.


## 2. Aim of the study

To produce an updated SC-CO₂ estimate that responds fully to the NASEM near-term recommendations, using improved socioeconomic projections, climate models, damage functions and discounting.


## 3. What the authors did

They built the open-source Greenhouse Gas Impact Value Estimator (GIVE) on the Mimi.jl platform. It has four steps: 1. Socioeconomics: RFF-SPs, probabilistic multi-century projections of country population and GDP per capita, and global CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O emissions. 2. Climate: the FaIR climate model and the BRICK sea-level model, with parameter uncertainty. 3. Damages: sectoral, regional damage functions for agriculture, temperature-related mortality, building energy use and sea-level rise (CIAM, with optimal coastal adaptation). 4. Discounting: a Ramsey-like discount rate linked to uncertain economic growth; preferred near-term rate 2% (ρ = 0.2%, η = 1.24). They compared results step by step with the DICE-2016R model and tested two meta-analysis-based global damage functions as a sensitivity analysis.

### 4.2 Damages by sector

Temperature-related mortality is the largest driver (mean partial SC-CO₂ $90, range $39–$165), followed by agriculture ($84, range −$23 to $263, the widest because CO₂ fertilisation may bring benefits). Building energy use adds $9 (cooling offset by lower heating) and sea-level rise only $2, because its damages come late and are reduced by optimal coastal adaptation (p. 690).

### 4.4 Socioeconomic outlook

In the RFF-SPs, median world population peaks at about 11 billion around 2130 and falls to 7.3 billion by 2300; median per-capita growth averages 0.88% a year over 2020–2300; and median net CO₂ emissions fall to 17 GtCO₂ in 2100, about 40% of today's level (p. 689).


## 5. Conclusions

5. Updated, comprehensive evidence implies a much higher SC-CO₂ than values used in policy today. 6. The lower near-term discount rate is the largest driver; updated damage functions are second. 7. Higher SC-CO₂ values substantially raise the estimated benefits of mitigation and the net benefits of stricter climate policy. 8. The estimate is probably conservative because several damage types and tipping points are missing. 9. The method can be extended to other years and to CH₄, N₂O and HFCs.


## 6. Limitations

- Only four damage sectors; biodiversity, labour productivity, conflict, migration and most non-market damages are missing; only one tipping point (rapid Antarctic ice loss) is approximated.
- Results are very sensitive to the discount rate.
- Values are global, in 2020 US dollars, with no equity weighting; one climate model (FaIR) is used.
- For Indian village work the value must be converted carefully and reported as a range.
