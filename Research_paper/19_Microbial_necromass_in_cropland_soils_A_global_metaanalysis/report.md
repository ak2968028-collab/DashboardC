#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Across 481 paired observations from 61 articles, cropland management raised microbial necromass carbon by 12% (N fertiliser), 14% (cover crops), 20% (no or reduced tillage), 21% (manure) and 21% (straw); biochar had no effect. Straw worked best in semi-arid and cool climates and soils with pH < 8; NT/RT in warm, humid areas; manure everywhere. Effects were absent in sandy soils and short trials (≤3 years). Necromass made up 45–52% of the SOC gain under the main practices.


## 1. Background

Microbial necromass, the remains of dead bacteria and fungi, is a large and persistent part of soil organic carbon (SOC); in this dataset it was about 52% of SOC (AM p. 26). It is measured with amino sugar biomarkers: muramic acid comes only from bacteria, while glucosamine comes mainly from fungi (AM pp. 7, 11). Management effects had been measured in single studies but not summarised globally (AM p. 2).


## 2. Aim of the study

To find how N fertiliser, manure, straw, biochar, no or reduced tillage (NT/RT) and cover crops change microbial necromass C, its contribution to SOC and its accumulation efficiency (necromass gain per unit SOC gain), and which climate and soil conditions favour accumulation (AM p. 7).


## 3. What the authors did

The authors selected 61 articles giving 481 paired observations, mostly from East Asia, North America and Western Europe (AM p. 10). Fungal and bacterial necromass C were calculated from glucosamine and muramic acid with standard conversion factors (AM p. 11). They calculated response ratios and

tested climate, soil pH, texture, initial SOC, straw amount and experiment duration with Random Forest and structural equation models (AM pp. 15–18).

- Only straw and NT/RT raised the share of SOC that is necromass (fungal share); manure raised the bacterial share but not the total share (AM p. 15).
- Manure raised necromass by 42% in warm climates, 28% in humid and 25% in semi-arid climates (AM p. 16).
- Manure and straw effects were larger when initial SOC was above 12 g/kg (AM p. 16).
- Effects were strongest in loam and clay soils and absent in sandy soils (AM p. 17).
- No significant effects in trials of 3 years or less, except manure; medium- to long-term trials showed the gains (AM p. 17).
- SOC rose 13–24% under these practices, and necromass made up 45–52% of the SOC increase, 9– 16% more than under N fertiliser (AM pp. 3, 26).

## 5. Conclusions

Long-term cropland management increases SOC by increasing microbial necromass. Getting the most necromass and SOC requires site-specific management matched to climate and soil (AM pp. 3, 28).


## 6. Limitations

- Amino sugar conversion factors give approximate necromass values (AM p. 11).
- Studies are concentrated in East Asia, North America and Western Europe (AM p. 10); few come from Indian rice-wheat systems (thesis author's assessment).
- Results are percentage changes, not sequestration rates per hectare.
- This pack uses the accepted manuscript; page numbers differ from the published version.
