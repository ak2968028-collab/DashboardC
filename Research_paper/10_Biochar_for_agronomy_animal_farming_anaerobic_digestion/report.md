#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Biochar, biomass heated with little oxygen, stores carbon for centuries if it is not burned or oxidised later; its final stores are soils, infrastructure and landfills. The review covers its uses across sectors. On farms, biochar-based fertilisers can raise nutrient use, 1% biochar in cattle diets cut enteric methane by 11–13% in vivo, biochar stabilises biogas digesters, and in compost it cuts methane and N₂O. Most evidence comes from single studies. Barriers are cost, poor kilns, weak policy and awareness; EBC and IBI standards exist (pp. 2385–2386, 2402–2403, 2461–2462).


## 1. Background

Carbon sequestration captures atmospheric carbon and stores it in a stable form. Biochar is a charcoal- like material made by pyrolysis of crop residues, wood or manure. It turns quickly decaying plant carbon into a stable form; a cited meta-analysis found a stable pool (97% of the carbon) with a mean residence time of about 556 years and a labile pool (3%) of 108 days (pp. 2385–2386). THE SINK RULE Biochar counts as a carbon sink only if it is not used for energy and is not thermally degraded or oxidised during or after use. Its final reservoirs are soils, civil infrastructure and landfills (p. 2386).


## 2. Aim of the study

To review  biochar  applications that  act  as carbon  sinks, in  agronomy, animal  farming, anaerobic digestion, composting, environmental remediation, construction and energy storage, and to discuss barriers to large-scale use (p. 2386).


## 3. What the authors did

The authors reviewed a large body of literature and summarised it in 21 tables covering standards, biochar-based fertilisers, feed trials, digestion experiments, composting, pollutant removal, cement and asphalt, and battery electrodes. This summary focuses on soil, animals, biogas and compost, which matter most for the thesis.


## 4. Main findings


### 4.1 Agronomy

Biochar contains few nutrients, so using it as a fertiliser alone needs 10–50 t/ha, which is costly; rates above 50 t/ha harm soil microbes and germination. It is mainly used as an amendment, or at about 1 t/ha as a nutrient carrier. In one tropical study, biochar-based N fertilisers raised corn yield by about 26% over urea alone. Reported effects on soil GHG emissions are inconsistent (pp. 2387, 2395).

### 4.3 Anaerobic digestion and composting

- In digesters, biochar buffers volatile fatty acids, reduces ammonia inhibition, supports microbial biofilms and electron transfer, and can raise methane yield and upgrade biogas by adsorbing CO₂ (pp. 2404–2409).
- In compost, biochar cut methane by up to 80% (wheat straw and sewage sludge, 12% biochar) and up to 32% (chicken manure), and reduced N₂O; effects on CO₂ were conflicting (–26.1% to +53.2%) (pp. 2419–2420).

### 4.4 Other uses

Biochar can remove pollutants from water and soil, improve cement, asphalt and building composites, and serve as a precursor for battery and supercapacitor electrodes, though these often need 700 °C or more and chemical activation (pp. 2385, 2460–2461).


## 5. Conclusions

1. Biochar-based carbon removal adds value in many sectors beyond the carbon it stores, if the final use keeps the carbon intact and follows standards (p. 2461). 2. Poorly built stoves and drum kilns in developing countries can raise GHG and toxic emissions; feedstock drying adds cost (p. 2461). 3. Wider use needs awareness among the public and governments, supporting policy, and universal standards beyond EBC and IBI (pp. 2461–2462).


## 6. Limitations

- The review is very broad and summarises other studies, often lab-scale or short-term; many numbers come from a single study.
- Soil GHG effects are reported as inconsistent (p. 2387).
- There are few Indian field examples, and smallholder costs are not worked out (thesis author's assessment).
