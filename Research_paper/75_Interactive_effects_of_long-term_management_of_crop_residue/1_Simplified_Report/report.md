#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

In a rice–wheat experiment running since 2008 at Ludhiana, keeping crop residues instead of removing or burning them improved soil structure, nutrients, microbes and enzymes. With both rice and wheat straw retained (T5, Happy Seeder zero-till wheat), wheat yield without any phosphorus (6.10 t/ha) was on par with 30 and 60 kg P₂O₅/ha, and T5 gave the highest mean yield (6.19 t/ha). Burning gave lower yields (5.52 t/ha mean). Some abstract figures, including the 73.9% SOC rise, do not match the body of the paper.


## 1. Background

More than 80% of rice straw in the region is burned because it has little value and the window before wheat is short. Residues influence soil phosphorus availability, but P advice under long-term residue management was lacking (pp. 1–2).


## 2. Aim of the study

To study how long-term crop residue management interacts with phosphorus levels to affect wheat productivity, P requirement and soil health.


## 3. What the authors did

Six residue and tillage practices (T1–T6) established in 2008 formed the main plots. After 11 years, three P levels (0, 30, 60 kg P₂O₅/ha) were added as sub-plots for 2019–20 and 2020–21. Soil physical, chemical and biological properties and wheat yield were measured (Table 2).

## 5. Conclusions

1. Residue retention or incorporation improves soil physical, chemical and biological properties. 2. With rice residue alone, half the recommended P (30 kg) gave yield equal to the full dose. 3. With both residues retained, no P fertiliser was needed; P doses should be adjusted to residue practice.


## 6. Limitations

- One sandy loam site in Punjab; only two seasons of the P study.
- No greenhouse gas or SOC stock measurements.
- Several abstract and text figures conflict with the tables.
