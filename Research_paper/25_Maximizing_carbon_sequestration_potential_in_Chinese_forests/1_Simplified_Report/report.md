#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

The authors drive a process-based model (DLEM) with China's national forest inventory to project forest biomass carbon from 2020 to 2100, including wood harvest, forest age and the official planting plan. Biomass carbon rises by 13.6 ± 1.5 Pg C, a mean sink of 172.3 ± 16.9 Tg C per year, mostly in natural non- timber forests. Ignoring how harvest changes forest age puts the sink peak 1–3 decades too early. Wood products add 0.6–2.0 Pg C, and optimal management (longer rotations and replacing unsuitable species) adds 2.3 ± 0.03 Pg C, but only if started quickly.


## 1. Background

China has the largest planted forest area in the world and pledged to expand it by 49.5 Mha from 2021, after 15 Mha planned for 2016–2020 (pp. 1, 9–10). Published estimates of its future forest carbon sink diverge because long-term data are limited, accounting methods differ, and most studies leave out forest demography (age structure) and wood harvest (pp. 1–2). The spread of reported stocks rises from 9% in 2020 to 25% in 2060 (p. 3).


## 2. Aim of the study

To project China's forest biomass carbon stock and sink from 2020 to 2100 with tree demography, harvest, species choice and forest structure considered together, and to assess how much improved management could add (p. 2).


## 3. What the authors did

The study uses more than 18 million tree records from about 415,000 permanent inventory plots (1999– 2018); the 9th inventory (2014–2018) gives the validated starting stock, and over 50,000 plots give age

dynamics (pp. 2–3, 6). DLEM runs on a 0.5° grid with harvest limited to timber forests and set by species and age, under four SSP climate and CO₂ scenarios and tree survival of 47% or 85% for new plantings (pp. 3, 8, 10). New plantations follow the national plan to 2050 on grassland and shrubland only, with species chosen from habitat-suitability maps (p. 10). A statistical model is used for comparison. Biomass carbon excludes dead wood, litter and soil (p. 2).


## 4. Main findings

- Biomass carbon stock rises by 13.6 ± 1.5 Pg C from 2020 to 2100, to 21.6–24.3 Pg C in 2100; raising survival of new plantings from 47% to 85% adds 1.0–1.1 Pg C (p. 3).
- The mean sink is 172.3 ± 16.9 Tg C per year over 2020–2100: 0.201 Pg C per year in 2020–2060 and 0.143 in 2060–2100 (pp. 4–5).
- Natural non-timber forests provide 57.7% of the stock increase, planted non-timber forests 34.2% and timber forests 8.1%; existing forests give 70.4 ± 3% and new forests 29.6 ± 2% (p. 4).
- The statistical model puts the sink peak in the 2020s–2040s; the process model, with harvest and real age change, in the 2050s. Ignoring harvest effects on age shifts the peak 1–3 decades too early (pp. 1, 5).
- Harvest removes 7.8 ± 0.3 Pg C from timber forests in 2020–2100; the wood product pool reaches 1.9 ± 0.1 Pg C in 2100, limited by the 61.5% share of short-lived paper. More long-lived products would raise the pool by 75.3% (1.4 ± 0.08 Pg C) (p. 5).
- Postponing harvest by 5 years after the key year adds 4.3–5.6 Tg C per year in timber forests; replacing ~4.13 Mha of unsuitable species adds 0.17–1.1 Tg C per year. Each year of delay loses 11– 17 Tg C (p. 5, Table 1). Together the paper reports an extra 28.1 ± 0.4 Tg C per year, 2.3 ± 0.03 Pg C in total (p. 5).

## 5. Conclusions

China's forests will remain a substantial net sink to 2100, but projections must include harvest and forest demography. Long-lived wood products and optimal management can raise the sink, and the authors urge swift implementation because delayed action gives little benefit (pp. 5–6).


## 6. Limitations

- China-wide modelling of national forestry; no village-scale or farm-tree data (0.5° grid).
- Biomass carbon only; soil, litter and dead wood are excluded (p. 2).
- Results depend on assumed survival, harvest rules and SSP scenarios.
- Thesis author's assessment: the process model is not directly usable in an M.Tech study, and species and climate differ from the Varuna basin.
