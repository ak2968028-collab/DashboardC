#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Combining 1.19 million forest inventory plots with three satellite biomass maps, the authors estimate that forests hold 328 Gt C (221–472) less carbon than they would naturally. Ground and satellite results differ by only 12% globally. Outside urban and agricultural land the potential is 226 Gt C (151–363): 61% (139 Gt C) in existing degraded forests that can recover if protected, and 39% (87 Gt C) where forests were removed or fragmented. Forests cannot replace emission cuts, but conserving and restoring diverse forests, led by local communities, can help (pp. 92, 96–98).


## 1. Background

Forests hold 80–90% of global plant biomass and much of land biodiversity. Humans have removed almost half of Earth's natural forests, and deforestation still releases 0.9–2.3 Gt C per year, about 15% of annual human carbon emissions (p. 92). Earlier satellite-based estimates of extra forest carbon potential ranged from 200 to 300 Gt C, and critics suggested  they  could  be  4–5  times  too  high.  No  independent  ground-based  estimate  existed  to benchmark them (p. 92).


## 2. Aim of the study

To estimate the natural forest carbon potential, the carbon forests could hold without extensive human degradation under current climate, by combining ground-sourced and satellite-derived approaches, and to split the gap between existing forests and deforested land (pp. 92–93).


## 3. What the authors did

- Ground-sourced (GS): the GFBI database of 1,188,771 plots (1,089,026 kept after quality control, about 25.8 million trees) with DBH for each tree, converted to biomass with allometric equations and mapped with random-forest models (p. 93; Methods).
- Satellite-derived (SD): three biomass maps (ESA-CCI, Walker et al. and a harmonised product) (p. 93).
- Natural state: type 1 models set human-disturbance variables to zero; type 2 models learn only from undisturbed areas, giving 4 GS and 6 SD models (p. 93).
- Total carbon: tree carbon scaled up with root fractions, soil carbon (0–2 m), dead wood and litter, and compared with a meta-analysis of earlier estimates (pp. 93, 95–96).

## 4. Main findings


### 4.1 Agreement between methods

GS and SD estimates of potential differed by only 12% globally. Existing live tree carbon was 367 Gt C (GS) and 394 Gt C (SD), under 7% apart; average R² between the two was 0.72. SD models predicted higher potential in 82% of pixels, especially in South American tropics (pp. 92, 94–95).


### 4.2 Living tree carbon

Potential living tree carbon is about 600 Gt C (487–712) in a natural canopy area of 4.4 billion ha; the gap to current stocks is 217 Gt C (153–267): tropical 123, temperate 55, dryland 25 and boreal 14 Gt C. This lies at the lower end of earlier estimates (150–446 Gt C) and is probably conservative (pp. 95–97).

## 5. Conclusions

1. Forests cannot substitute for emission reductions, but conserving, restoring and sustainably managing diverse forests can help meet climate and biodiversity targets (p. 92). 2. Most of the realistic gain (61%) comes from letting existing degraded forests recover, with few land conflicts (pp. 96–98). 3. Restoration must be socially responsible and led by local and indigenous communities; agroforestry and similar approaches can provide livelihoods (pp. 97, 99).


## 6. Limitations

- Detailed local land use is not resolved, so social and economic limits may reduce the real potential (p. 98).
- The estimate is biophysical, based on 1979–2013 climate, and ignores future human pressure, warming, drought, fire and CO₂ fertilisation (pp. 98–99).
- Reduced surface albedo from forests warms high latitudes (p. 97).
- Tropical ground data are sparse; soil carbon dominates uncertainty at high latitudes (pp. 95–96, 98).
- Cropland and urban land, most of the Varuna basin, are outside the 226 Gt C estimate.
