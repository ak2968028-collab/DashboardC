#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Soil carbon is promoted as a win-win for climate and food. The authors show that almost no global estimates include saturation; adding it cuts the potential contribution by 53–81% to 2100. Gains can also reverse, and across more than 21 meta-analyses the yield effect of more SOC is negative, neutral or positive. They argue against global SOC targets for farm soils and for soil-smart, locally adapted agriculture, with SOC gains treated as a co-benefit.


## 1. Background

Keeping warming below 1.5 °C needs net-zero CO₂ within decades, and nearly all 1.5 °C scenarios rely on CO₂ removal (p. 1). Soil organic carbon (SOC) sequestration is promoted by high-level initiatives such as 4p1000 (launched at COP21 in 2015) as a way to help both the climate and food security (p. 2). IPCC WG3 (2022) estimated 1.8–4.1 Gt CO₂ per year for croplands, grasslands, agroforestry and biochar, and ranked SOC sequestration fourth among options in 2030 (pp. 3–4).


## 2. Aim of the study

To critically re-examine whether global SOC sequestration strategies deliver both climate mitigation and food production benefits, by accounting for saturation and reviewing the evidence on SOC and crop yields (p. 1).


## 3. What the authors did

The  authors  reviewed  top-down  (recovering  historical  losses  or  plant  inputs)  and  bottom-up (extrapolating measured rates) estimates of global potential. Starting from Bossio et al.'s 3.3 Gt CO₂/yr, they recalculated cumulative sequestration to 2100 under a constant rate, a slowly decreasing rate and a rapidly decreasing rate (30-year half-life), and compared it with the 1.5 °C budget (pp. 3–5). They reviewed more than 21 meta-analyses on SOC and yield (Table 1, pp. 7–8) and discussed measurement problems (p. 3).


## 4. Main findings


### 4.1 Climate potential

- Ignoring saturation overestimates potential by 53–81% (p. 4).
- Historical soil carbon loss estimates range from 40 Gt C (148 Gt CO₂) to 537 Gt C (1,989 Gt CO₂); the latest is 116 Gt C (430 Gt CO₂) (p. 3).
- The input-based estimate of 0.52 Gt CO₂/yr (Janzen et al., 2022) implies an insignificant contribution (0–1%) (p. 5).
- The total net emission reduction needed for 1.5 °C is about 3000 Gt CO₂ (p. 5).
- SOC is reversible and shorter-lived than fossil carbon, so it cannot substitute for or delay emission cuts (p. 4). 4.2 Yields and measurement
- Across more than 21 meta-analyses, yield effects of more SOC range from negative to positive (p. 1, Table 1).
- Neutral or negative effects are found much more often when studies correct for N, P and K supply; most positive results come from studies where extra nutrients were included (p. 9).
- Benefits are larger on sandy soils, and one study found a positive effect in tropical climates (p. 9).
- The yield effect of SOC levels off at about 2% SOC (p. 9).
- Many studies report concentrations, not stocks; depth is reported inconsistently; equivalent soil mass methods are more accurate (p. 3).

## 5. Conclusions

The evidence does not justify global agendas that put SOC sequestration first. The authors call for 'soil- smart agriculture', adapted to each local context and measuring many soil functions together, and for moving away from global SOC targets in agricultural soils. SOC sequestration should be regarded as a co- benefit (p. 1). They also see better nitrogen use efficiency as a missed opportunity to cut N₂O, since agriculture causes about 70% of human N₂O emissions (p. 2).


## 6. Limitations

- Opinion paper; recalculations rely on published estimates and simple saturation assumptions.
- No new field data and no India-specific analysis.
