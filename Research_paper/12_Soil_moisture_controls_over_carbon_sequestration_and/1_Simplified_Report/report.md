#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Soil moisture controls photosynthesis, respiration, microbial activity and organic matter dynamics, so it governs carbon sequestration and GHG emissions. In the compiled studies, CO₂ shows a 'Peak and Decline' pattern at about 40% WFPS, while CH₄ peaks at 60–80% and N₂O near 80% WFPS, with wide site variation. Drought cuts uptake; floods favour methane. Conservation agriculture, agroforestry and optimised water management (AWD can cut paddy CH₄ by up to 50%) help. Better sensing, hybrid models and long-term policy incentives are needed (pp. 1, 4–9).


## 1. Background

Soil moisture affects plant photosynthesis and respiration, soil microbes, and the breakdown and stabilisation of soil organic matter. Variability in soil moisture accounts for about 90% of interannual variability in global land carbon uptake, and soil moisture limits cut annual photosynthesis by about 15% globally. Drying trends may reduce the land sink by 2–3 Gt C per year (pp. 2–4). Moderate moisture favours storage, but drought and flooding disrupt it. Flooded soils lose oxygen, favouring CH₄ and N₂O; dry soils limit growth and microbial activity (pp. 1, 3).


## 2. Aim of the study

To synthesise how soil moisture regulates carbon sequestration and GHG emissions (CS-GHG) across ecosystems,  identify  moisture  thresholds,  review  how  models  represent  moisture,  and  assess management practices and policies (p. 2).


## 3. What the authors did

The authors reviewed field and lab studies, compiled the effect of moisture on carbon sequestration by location and land cover (Table 1) and the correlation patterns and peak-emission WFPS thresholds for CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O (Table 2), compared eight ecosystem models (Table 3), and discussed observation methods and policy (pp. 4–8).


## 4. Main findings


### 4.1 Mechanisms

- Root (autotrophic) respiration is more sensitive than microbial respiration: drought cut its share of soil respiration from 33% to 16% in a subtropical forest and from 66% to 35% in dry grasslands (p. 3).
- Aerobic soils mainly emit CO₂; waterlogged soils favour denitrification (N₂O) and methanogenesis (CH₄) (p. 3).
- CO₂ production was 31–40% higher at 65% than at 45% water holding capacity (p. 3). 4.2 Moisture thresholds for emissions Table A. Drying-rewetting cycles raise CO₂ emissions by 35.7% on average, and freeze-thaw cycles can raise CO₂ up to 1.7 times and N₂O up to 5.8 times. Floods favour CH₄ (pp. 4–5). 4.4 Management and policy
- Conservation agriculture (residue cover, legumes, cover crops, mulching, reduced tillage) keeps moisture and raises soil carbon; practices should be location-specific (pp. 6–7).
- Agroforestry maintains soil moisture and adds carbon inputs (p. 7).
- In rice, AWD can cut CH₄ by up to 50% compared with continuous flooding; irrigation scheduling and drip irrigation avoid waterlogging (p. 7).
- Policy should move from short-term yields to long-term carbon, using subsidies, payments for ecosystem services, carbon credits with robust MRV, and local soil information systems (p. 8).

## 5. Conclusions

1. Soil moisture is pivotal: optimal levels raise sequestration, extremes reduce it (p. 9). 2. Gas-specific thresholds call for targeted moisture management (p. 9). 3. Conservation agriculture, agroforestry and optimised water management are key; predictions need high-resolution sensing, ground networks and hybrid models within a model-experiment (ModEx) loop (pp. 8–9).


## 6. Limitations

- Thresholds are general patterns from varied studies and differ by soil and site (pp. 4–5).
- The AWD figure is an upper value from cited studies, not a mean (p. 7).
- Models still represent moisture effects and human management imperfectly (pp. 6, 8).
- Few Indian or rice-wheat studies are included (thesis author's assessment).
