#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

From 1144 soil profiles, the authors estimate that mineral-associated organic carbon (MOC) totals 899 Pg C to 1 m, 66–70% of soil carbon, but only 42% (topsoil) and 21% (subsoil) of what soil minerals could hold. Croplands and subsoils are the most undersaturated. If croplands reached natural-land saturation they could gain about 104 Pg C, and the least saturated soils accrue carbon about three times faster.


## 1. Background

Soil holds more carbon than the atmosphere and vegetation combined, and human land use has caused a large net loss of soil carbon over two centuries (p. 2). More than half of soil organic carbon is bound chemically or physically to minerals. This mineral-associated organic carbon (MOC) is hard for microbes to reach, so it can persist up to 1000 times longer (100–10,000 years) than particulate organic carbon (POC), the loosely held plant debris (p. 2). KEY TERMS MOCmax is the maximum MOC a soil's minerals can hold, set by the amount and type of clay and silt. %C saturation = MOC ÷ MOCmax. The carbon deficit = MOCmax – MOC (pp. 2–5).


## 2. Aim of the study

To estimate global MOC stocks and the mineralogical capacity to store MOC, map undersaturation by land use and depth, and test whether soils further from capacity gain carbon faster after management changes (p. 2).


## 3. What the authors did

- Synthesised MOC (by size and density fractionation), POC and SOC from 1144 soil profiles spanning clay + silt of 1.5–100%, mean annual temperature of −2.9 to 29 °C and rainfall of 79–3806 mm/yr (p. 2).
- Classified soils as high-activity (illite, smectite, vermiculite, chlorite) or low-activity (kaolinite, gibbsite) minerals and fitted MOCmax as the 95th quantile of MOC against clay + silt (pp. 2–3).
- Used random forest models to study climate and input effects (R² = 0.60) and to map current MOC globally at 0.5° (R² = 0.79), excluding tundra, peatlands and deserts (pp. 3–4).
- Synthesised 103 carbon-accrual measurements following management interventions lasting 2–65 years (p. 6).

## 4. Main findings


### 4.1 Mineral capacity

MOCmax was 86 ± 9 mg C per g mineral for high-activity and 48 ± 6 for low-activity mineral soils. There is no universal linear MOC–clay relationship; mineral type must be considered, and studies that ignored it gave much lower capacities (pp. 2–3).


### 4.2 Saturation by land use and depth

Soils averaged 40% saturation. Natural ecosystems (forest, grassland) averaged 46%, agricultural systems only 31%; surface soils were 43% and deeper soils 19% saturated. Grasslands were closer to saturation than forests at depth, showing the role of roots (pp. 3–4).

### 4.4 Climate effects

MOC declined with mean annual temperature, suggesting a global vulnerability of MOC to warming; soils far from saturation were more temperature-sensitive than near-saturated soils, so restoring degraded soils may also make them more resilient to warming (p. 4). Aboveground litter was a weak predictor of MOC (p. 3).


### 4.5 Sequestration efficiency

Across 103 accrual measurements, soils furthest from capacity accrued carbon fastest: rates averaged three times higher at sites with <10% saturation than at 50% saturation. Hotspots lie in extensive cropping regions such as the Midwestern United States and India. Average accrual over all croplands could store over 1 Pg C per year for a time, but rates fall as soils fill up (p. 6).


## 5. Conclusions

1. MOC makes up most (69%) of soil carbon in the top metre of non-permafrost mineral soils but less than half of the mineralogical capacity. 2. The degree of undersaturation indicates sequestration efficiency over years to decades and can guide where soil carbon management works best. 3. Long-cropped, grazed, abandoned and degraded lands are priority areas; practices that store carbon deeper (e.g. deep-rooted vegetation) are promising. 4. Incentives and valuation of soil carbon are needed to scale action across more than 500 million farms (p. 6).


## 6. Limitations

- MOCmax is a theoretical ceiling; climate often prevents soils from reaching it, and it is not a recommended target (p. 5).
- Global maps are at 0.5° resolution and exclude tundra, peatlands, deserts and organic soils (Histosols) (pp. 4, 7).
- Low-activity mineral soils are represented by far fewer measurements (93) than high-activity soils (1303) (Fig. 1).
- High accrual rates decline as soils approach saturation, so gains are time-limited (p. 6).
- Our assessment: the maps are not calibrated for Indo-Gangetic alluvial soils, and MOC fractionation data are not part of routine Indian soil testing.
