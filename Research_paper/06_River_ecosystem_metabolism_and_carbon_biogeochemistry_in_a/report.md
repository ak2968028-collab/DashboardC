#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Rivers are active biogeochemical reactors. Land delivers about 3.2 Pg C/yr to rivers (0.72 organic, 2.44 inorganic), roughly a quarter of terrestrial NEP. Rivers are strongly heterotrophic: they respire about 37% of the incoming organic carbon and emit 1.9–2.3 Pg C/yr of CO₂, over 70% from the tropics, and only about a quarter of the land carbon reaches the coast. Land use, dams and climate change are altering these fluxes, so the authors call for a global River Observing System.


## 1. Background

Rivers were long considered pipes that move carbon from land to the ocean. Today they are understood as biogeochemical reactors that metabolise organic carbon, emit CO₂ and CH₄ and bury carbon in sediments, floodplains and deltas. The IPCC and the Global Carbon Project include rivers in their budgets, but usually as a black box, which prevents prediction of how global change will alter river carbon fluxes (AM pp. 2–3). KEY TERMS GPP (gross primary production) is carbon fixed in the river; ER (ecosystem respiration) is carbon respired by all organisms; NEP = GPP – ER. Negative NEP means the river is heterotrophic and is subsidised by carbon from land (AM pp. 4–5).


## 2. Aim of the study

To  review  river  ecosystem  metabolism  research,  synthesise  best  estimates  of  river  metabolism worldwide, quantify organic and inorganic carbon flows from land through rivers to the coast, discuss

global-change impacts, and set research priorities, including a global River Observing System (AM pp. 2, 4).


## 3. What the authors did

The authors summarised published metabolism budgets for tropical, temperate and high-latitude rivers and built a global mass balance of river carbon fluxes, including CH₄ and reservoir emissions and burial. Uncertainty follows IPCC guidelines, with lower and upper bounds from sensitivity analyses (AM pp. 6–8).

Over  70% of  river  CO₂  is  emitted  in  the  tropics,  and  low-order,  high-slope  streams  contribute disproportionately (AM p. 9).


### 4.3 Global change

- Terrestrial subsidies: higher NPP and wetter climates deliver more carbon to rivers, mostly recycled to CO₂; Earth system models predict accelerating delivery this century (AM pp. 14–15).
- Land use: deforestation and peatland conversion mobilise old carbon; agricultural soils are a major source of OC and nutrients and alter river GPP and ER (AM pp. 15–16).
- Flow regimes: drying makes non-perennial rivers more heterotrophic and can promote CH₄; flashier flows reduce GPP and ER (AM pp. 16–17).
- Dams: reservoirs can emit large amounts of CO₂ and CH₄, with methane produced in deep anoxic water; CO₂ evasion from Chinese rivers fell 32% over three decades, partly attributed to damming (AM pp. 17–18).
- Deoxygenation: eutrophication can switch rivers to denitrification and methanogenesis, producing N₂O and CH₄ (AM p. 18).

## 5. Conclusions

1. River networks are proficient at retaining, metabolising and emitting terrestrial carbon and matter for continental carbon budgets. 2. Global change will alter the timing and location of river carbon storage and emissions in complex ways. 3. A global River Observing System (RIOS), combining sensor networks, field surveys, satellites, scaling laws and models, is needed, with more multi-year time series from the tropics, high latitudes and mountains and funding that includes the Global South (AM pp. 18–20).


## 6. Limitations

- Many fluxes are poorly constrained; reservoir fluxes could be up to an order of magnitude larger, and high-flow exports are undersampled (AM pp. 10–12).
- Sensor records are biased toward temperate rivers; tropical data are scarce (AM p. 19).
- This summary uses the accepted manuscript; page numbers differ from the printed Nature article.
- Our assessment: the review is global and gives no Indian or basin-specific values, so it is context rather than an emission factor source.
