#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Over 2010–2019 the world emitted about 575 Tg CH₄ per year (atmospheric inversions), 65% of it directly from human activity. Bottom-up estimates are higher (669 Tg), but for the first time the two ranges overlap. Agriculture and waste is the largest human source group, livestock the largest single source, the tropics give 64% of emissions, and South Asia is the third-largest region. Emissions reached 608 Tg in 2020 and have tracked no-policy scenarios since 2012.


## 1. Background

Methane (CH₄) is the second most important greenhouse gas influenced by humans, after CO₂. Its mean atmospheric concentration reached 1912 ppb in 2022, 2.6 times the estimated pre-industrial value of 1750, and both emissions and concentrations have risen again since 2007 after a temporary pause (pp. 1875–1876).

Methane stays in the air for a shorter time than CO₂ but has a stronger radiative effect. The paper gives its 100-year global warming potential (GWP-100) as 27 for methane from microbial sources and 29.8 for fossil methane; over 20 years the values are 79.7 and 82.5 (p. 1876, from Forster et al. 2021).


> **WHY THE BUDGET IS HARD:**

Sources such as wetlands, lakes and rivers, livestock, rice paddies, landfills, fossil fuels and fires overlap in space, and the main sink, destruction by short-lived hydroxyl (OH) radicals, is uncertain and variable. The Global Carbon Project therefore brings scientists together to update the budget regularly (p. 1875).


## 2. Aim of the study

This is the third edition of the living review on the global methane budget (after Saunois et al. 2016, 2020). It aims to:

- estimate all CH₄ sources and sinks for 2010–2019, 2000–2009 and the year 2020;
- compare top-down (atmospheric) and bottom-up (source-by-source) estimates and explain their differences;
- give budgets by latitude band, by 18 regions and by source sector;
- improve inland freshwater estimates and, for the first time, estimate double counting with wetlands and the human-caused part of these natural fluxes.

## 3. What the authors did

Top-down: an ensemble of  24 inversion runs works backwards from methane measured at surface stations and by the GOSAT satellite to the emissions needed to explain it.  Bottom-up: emissions are added up source by source from anthropogenic inventories (EDGAR, CEDS, GAINS, USEPA, FAO-CH₄), 16 process-based wetland models, fire products, and data-driven estimates for lakes, ponds, reservoirs, rivers and geological seeps. Sinks come from chemistry–climate models and soil-uptake estimates (pp. 1875–1876, Sects. 2–4). Fluxes are reported in Tg CH₄ per year (1 Tg = 1 million tonnes). Uncertainty is the minimum–maximum range across the studies used, after removing outliers (Table 3 caption, p. 1885).

### 4.2 Trend and the year 2020

Emissions in 2000–2009 were 32 Tg/yr lower (range 9–40) than in 2010–2019. The year 2020 had the highest emissions of the period, 608 Tg CH₄/yr (581–627), 12% above the 2000s average. Since 2012, direct anthropogenic emissions have followed the IPCC scenarios that assume no or minimal climate policy (SSP5 and SSP3) (p. 1875). Atmospheric growth was 15.2 ppb/yr in 2020, 17.8 in 2021 and 14 in 2022 (pp. 1925–1926).


### 4.3 Where methane comes from

Top-down, the tropics (90° S–30° N) give 64% of emissions (364 Tg/yr), the mid-latitudes 32% (187 Tg/yr) and the high northern latitudes above 60° N about 4% (p. 1916). Southeast Asia (63 Tg/yr), China (57) and South Asia (52 [43–60]) are the top three regions and together emit 30% of the global total (p. 1917).

### 4.4 Natural sources and sinks

Combined wetland and inland freshwater emissions average  248 Tg/yr [159–369] bottom-up, with possible double counting of about 23 Tg/yr. Natural emissions, especially from wetlands and inland freshwaters, remain the largest source of uncertainty. Tropospheric chemical loss, the main sink, is 563 Tg/yr, with about 25% uncertainty; most inversions use the same OH field, so their sink ranges are likely too narrow (p. 1876).


## 5. Conclusions

1. For 2010–2019, global CH₄ emissions are 575 Tg/yr top-down; about 65% are anthropogenic (range 63–68%). 2. Bottom-up totals are larger, mostly because of larger and more uncertain natural emissions and likely double counting, but for the first time the two budgets agree within their uncertainty ranges. 3. Tropical emissions dominate (~64%), compared with mid-latitudes (~32%) and high northern latitudes (~4%). 4. The authors list five priorities: a high-resolution map of CH₄-emitting wet areas, process models for inland waters, more observations from local to global scales, better transport and sink representation in inversions, and inclusion of satellite-detected super-emitters in inventories. 5. The budget will be updated as a living review about every 3–4 years (p. 1925).


## 6. Limitations

- Results are global, by latitude and by 18 large regions; there are no district or village values, and South Asia is treated as one region.
- Uncertainty ranges are wide, especially for natural sources; regional uncertainty can reach 40–60% (e.g. India) (p. 1877).
- Gridded anthropogenic inventories are updated only every 3 to 5 years, so recent years rely on extrapolation (p. 1879).
- Our assessment: the paper gives no breakdown by animal type, breed or feed, which village livestock calculations need.
