#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

This review of 150 empirical studies argues that carbon credit policies, which have focused on forests, wetlands and heavy industry, should include agroforestry. It sets out three credit types: removed emissions (tree planting), avoided emissions (not cutting trees) and reduced emissions (energy-efficient technology). Credit income could offset the cost of establishing trees, the main barrier for smallholders. India's 2014 National Agroforestry Policy, the world's first, already lists carbon credit schemes as a pathway. The review is qualitative and gives no prices or incomes.


## 1. Background

Carbon taxes and cap-and-trade can burden developing countries, and carbon accounting suffers from double counting  and weak verification. Carbon  credit policies have mostly  supported  forests and wetlands, even though agroforestry is a climate-smart practice used by many smallholders.


## 2. Aim of the study

To examine how carbon credit-based policies can drive sustainable agroforestry, especially in developing countries.


## 3. What the author did

A systematic review of 150 English-language empirical papers from Google Scholar, Web of Science and Scopus. The review describes the national agroforestry policies of India and Nepal, classifies emissions trading and credit schemes, summarises the main credit types, and lists credit schemes that promote agroforestry by country.


## 4. Main findings

- The most common schemes are government-led: China's CCER, the EU ETS and California's Global Warming Solutions Act, aimed at transport, steel and cement.
- Three credit types guide policy: avoided emissions (not cutting trees), reduced emissions (energy- efficient technology) and removed emissions (tree planting, carbon capture).
- Agroforestry fits removal and avoided-emission credits; examples come from India, Nepal, Ghana, Kenya, Costa Rica, the USA and the EU (Table 4).
- Establishment and management costs are a key barrier that credit income could offset.
- Challenges include weak monitoring and verification, double counting, permanence and smallholder access to markets. INDIA'S POLICY India's National Agroforestry Policy (2014) was the world's first. Its pathways include secure land tenure, certified planting material, simpler rules for farm trees, insurance and credit, and establishing carbon credit schemes for agroforestry.

## 5. Conclusions

1. Agroforestry should be brought into carbon credit schemes. 2. Using all three credit types would encourage smallholders to adopt agroforestry by offsetting its costs. 3. Co-benefits include jobs, technology transfer, energy access, livelihoods and better environmental quality; robust verification and market access are needed.


## 6. Limitations

- Qualitative: no credit prices, incomes or carbon amounts.
- English-only search in three databases; single author.
- Verification costs are named as a challenge but not quantified.
