#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

On 28 farms in six districts of Andhra Pradesh (2019–2020), side-by-side no-till plots compared Zero Budget Natural Farming (ZBNF), organic and conventional farming. Across 44 farm experiments, ZBNF yield z-scores were higher (0.58 vs −0.34 organic and −0.24 conventional), but the gain was significant only for groundnut and tomato and varied by district. ZBNF plots were wetter and cooler and had more earthworms, likely because only they were mulched. Most soil nutrients did not differ from conventional, and soil organic carbon did not change.


## 1. Background

India's roughly 159 Mha of arable land supports the world's second-largest farm output, but heavy synthetic fertiliser and pesticide use harms water, health and farm finances. Zero Budget Natural Farming (ZBNF) is a grassroots movement backed by Andhra Pradesh's extension programme, but its yield claims lacked controlled, statistically tested evidence.


## 2. Aim of the study

To test whether ZBNF changes yield and soil properties compared with organic and conventional farming, and to test three ZBNF claims: better soil moisture, enough nutrients without fertiliser, and more soil biological activity.


## 3. What the authors did

Three 6 × 6 m plots were set up on each of 28 farms across six districts and run in Kharif 2019, Rabi 2019–20 and Kharif 2020. Because of Covid-19, only 3 farms took part in all three seasons, 15 in two and 10 in one. ZBNF used Bijamrita, Jiwamrita (desi cow dung and urine) and mulch; organic used purchased manure and vermicompost without mulch; conventional used synthetic inputs. No plot was tilled. Yields were z-transformed to compare across crops and analysed with mixed-effects models.


## 4. Main findings

- The ZBNF effect was significant only for groundnut and tomato; groundnut kernel yield was about 30–40% higher.
- ZBNF beat both alternatives in Prakasam, Nellore and Kadapa; only conventional in Krishna; only organic in Anantapur. In Visakhapatnam conventional yields were higher but not significantly.
- ZBNF plots had higher soil moisture, lower soil temperature (not in every season) and more earthworms in all seasons.
- P, K and micronutrients mostly did not differ; in season 3 conventional plots had more extractable N.
- Soil organic carbon and bulk density did not change with treatment. CAUTION Only ZBNF plots were mulched, so the yield benefit cannot be split between mulch and the cow-based inputs. Results come from short, uneven participation (3 farms in all seasons) and mostly vegetables, pulses and groundnut, not rice or wheat.

## 5. Conclusions

1. ZBNF had no yield penalty in any district studied, and some districts saw a yield gain. 2. The benefit likely comes from mulch: wetter, cooler soil and more earthworms. 3. Long-term and landscape-scale trials, and tests of each ZBNF input, are needed.


## 6. Limitations

- Three seasons with uneven farm participation.
- Crop and district are confounded; benefit significant in two crops only.
- No GHG measurements; no rice–wheat data.
- All treatments untilled, unlike most farmer practice.
