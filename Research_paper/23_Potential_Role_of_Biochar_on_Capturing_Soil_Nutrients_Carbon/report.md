#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

This review summarises how feedstock and pyrolysis conditions shape biochar and its effects on soil nutrients, carbon and pollutants. High pyrolysis temperatures make biochar more aromatic and alkaline but may reduce nutrient availability, and different biochars release nutrients at different rates. Biochar can improve soil quality and store carbon, but effects are soil- and crop-specific and can be negative. The authors call for cheaper, better-controlled engineered biochar and testing with local farming communities.


## 1. Background

Biochar (BC) is a carbon-rich solid made by pyrolysis of biomass, usually at 300–800 °C with little or no oxygen (p. 6). It is studied as a soil amendment and as an adsorbent of pollutants (p. 2). Its properties depend on feedstock, residence time and pyrolysis conditions, and reported crop responses conflict: one cited review found yield changes from −36% to +31% (p. 2).


## 2. Aim of the study

To summarise recent studies on the biochar composition that controls carbon in soil, and on biochar's role in soil fertility, nutrient capture, carbon sequestration and remediation of contaminated soils (p. 1).


## 3. What the authors did

The authors reviewed literature on how feedstock and pyrolysis conditions shape biochar, how biochar holds and releases nutrients, how it stores carbon, how engineered biochar removes contaminants, and

its reported drawbacks. Results of individual studies are summarised in tables (pp. 3–11). No systematic search or meta-analysis was reported.


## 4. Main findings

- Higher pyrolysis temperatures raise aromatic carbon, pH and ash but lower yield, CEC and volatile matter; at 500–800 °C nutrient availability may fall (pp. 3–4).
- Nutrient release differs by feedstock and process; most biochars release little N, so N fertiliser should accompany biochar (p. 6).
- Wood biochar is more stable than crop-residue biochar because of its higher lignin content (p. 7).
- Biochar can immobilise heavy metals and adsorb organic and inorganic pollutants, though it is selective (pp. 7, 9).
- A cited study (Woolf et al.) estimated that scaling up biochar could cut net human GHG emissions by up to 12% through fossil-fuel substitution (p. 10).
- Cited single-study results include up to twofold root and plant biomass at a 20% biochar rate (p. 11) and 18–24% more legume nodulation (p. 3).

## 5. Conclusions

Biochar can help maintain soil health sustainably, but research should focus on standardising production, predicting field decay, rhizosphere interactions and adsorption–desorption capacities (pp. 11–12). An economically  feasible  method  of  producing  engineered  biochar  with  better-controlled  pyrolysis  is needed, and applications should be optimised with local farming communities (p. 1).


## 6. Limitations

- Reported drawbacks include about 200% more weeds at 15 t/ha, fungal declines of 11% and 66%, harm to earthworms from aged biochar, and soil- and crop-specific effects (p. 9).
- Many numbers come from single studies with different feedstocks and doses; the 20% rate is far above normal field rates.
- The review says low-temperature biochar shows lower soil degradability (p. 7), which conflicts with its statement that high-temperature biochar is more aromatic and recalcitrant (p. 4).
