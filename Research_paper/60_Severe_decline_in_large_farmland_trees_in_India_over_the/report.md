#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Using 3–5 m satellite imagery and deep learning, the authors mapped about 0.6 billion trees in India's cropland, urban and bare land and tracked them individually. About 11 ± 2% of large trees (mean crown 96 m²) seen in 2010/2011 had gone by 2018–2022, and 5.3 million large trees seen in 2018/2019 were gone by 2020–2022. Hotspots in Telangana and Maharashtra lost up to half their large trees. Villagers linked the losses to new water supplies, paddy expansion and the belief that field trees reduce yields.


## 1. Background

Farmland covers 56% of India and forest only 20%, so many of India's trees are scattered on farms, along roads and in hedgerows. These multipurpose trees (neem, mahua, Acacia nilotica, shisham, jamun and others) provide shade, fodder, fruit, fuelwood and income, and store carbon. Agroforestry is promoted as a natural climate solution, but existing on-farm trees are poorly monitored because satellites coarser than 10 m cannot see individual trees (pp. 860–861).


## 2. Aim of the study

To map individual farmland trees across India and track their disappearance between 2010/2011 and 2018–2022.


## 3. What the authors did

They built country-wide mosaics of RapidEye images (5 m) for 2010 and 2011 and PlanetScope images (3 m) for each year 2018–2022, and trained two deep-learning tree-detection models. Farmland was masked with the 10 m ESA WorldCover map; closed plantations were excluded. Tree crown centres were

matched between years within a 15 m buffer. A 2010/2011 tree not found in any 2018–2022 image, or a 2018/2019 tree not detected in all three years 2020–2022, was classed as disappeared. Only high- confidence losses were reported, so the estimates are conservative. In August 2023 they held 12 qualitative interviews with villagers in Telangana, Haryana, Kerala, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir.

Rainfall in the decade was above average in most areas, so climate was not the main cause. All interviewees confirmed large reductions in mature trees within and along fields, mainly because new boreholes allowed paddy expansion, and because farmers saw field trees as having low benefit and feared that the shade of large crowns such as neem would reduce yields. Several reported more block plantations. Fungi and climate were not perceived as causes. GROSS LOSS, NOT NET CHANGE The study counts only losses of large trees and masks out plantations, so it does not contradict official reports of a net increase in trees outside forests. The concern is that mature, ecologically valuable trees are being replaced by plantations of lower ecological value.


## 5. Conclusions

1. Large farmland trees have thinned considerably across India in about a decade, with severe hotspots in central India. 2. This is unsettling given the emphasis on agroforestry as a natural climate solution; protecting existing trees deserves attention, not only planting. 3. The method can monitor every large tree yearly at national scale, supporting policy, illegal-felling checks and restoration monitoring.


## 6. Limitations

- Small (< ~10 m² crown) and newly planted trees are not detected; net change is not reported.
- No carbon or biomass estimates.
- Only 12 interviews; drivers are indicative.
- Main losses are in central India, not the Varuna basin.
