#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

In a six-year field trial in the North China Plain, adding sweet potato, peanut or soybean to the wheat– maize double crop raised equivalent yield by up to 38%, cut field N₂O by 30–49%, stored 1.4–2.0 t C/ha/yr in soil to 90 cm and lowered net GHG emissions by 83–92%, while soil health scores rose 41– 59%. Scaled up, the authors estimate 107 Mt CO₂-eq/yr less and 20% more farmer income.


## 1. Background

Since the Green Revolution, intensive cereal farming has relied on synthetic fertilisers and pesticides that emit large amounts of greenhouse gases and degrade soils. China's crop production rose 74% in 1986– 2016 while synthetic fertiliser use rose by more than 300%; in 2019 China's food-related GHG emissions reached 2.4 Gt CO₂-eq, about half from crop production (p. 1). The North China Plain, which supplies about 23% of China's cereal food, is dominated by winter wheat– summer maize double cropping on 70% of its arable land (p. 2). Diversified rotations with legumes could partly replace synthetic N through biological N fixation (Fig. 1).


## 2. Aim of the study

To assess whether rotations diversified with cash crops, legumes, other cereals or forage can increase food production and farmer income, reduce GHG emissions and improve soil health compared with wheat–maize (p. 2).


## 3. What the authors did

A field experiment ran from October 2016 to October 2022 at the Luancheng Agro-Ecosystem Station, Hebei Province (37°50  N, 114°40  E), with a semi-humid monsoon climate (mean 14.7 °C; 472 mm rain ′ ′

per year). The loam soil started with pH 7.6, bulk density 1.49 g/cm³ and SOC 11.5 g/kg (pp. 8–9).

- Six rotations: wheat–maize (control, WM) and sweet potato (SpWM), peanut (PWM), soybean (SWM), spring maize (SmWM) or ryegrass–sweet sorghum (RSWM) followed by wheat–maize.
- N₂O and CH₄ measured weekly; indirect emissions from fertiliser, pesticide, diesel, electricity for irrigation, seed and labour.
- SOC stocks to 90 cm in 2016 and 2022; net GHG = indirect + direct (N₂O + CH₄) – ΔSOC × 44/12 (p. 10).
- Soil health scored with the Cornell Soil Health Assessment from ten soil properties; microbial diversity by DNA sequencing.

## 4. Main findings


### 4.1 Yield and income

The sweet potato rotation increased annual equivalent yield by 38% over wheat–maize (13,185 kg/ha/yr) and net income by 60%; peanut and soybean rotations raised income by 13–22%. Wheat–maize grown after non-cereal crops had 26–32% more grain. The all-cereal spring maize rotation lowered equivalent yield by 16% and protein yield by 23% (pp. 2–3).

### 4.3 Soil health and microbes

After six years the soil health score was highest for peanut (59.5), soybean (56.8) and sweet potato (52.9) rotations, 41–59% above wheat–maize (33.2). Peanut soils had 6.5% more SOC and 29.7% more DOC. Microbial Shannon diversity rose 7–10% in these three rotations but not in the cereal systems; legumes stimulated soil microbial activity and raised SOC stocks by about 8% (pp. 1, 4–5).


### 4.4 Scaling up (authors' estimates)

Across the North China Plain, diversified systems could cut net emissions by 106.8 ± 31.7 Mt CO₂-eq per year (5.6% of China's food-system GHG), mainly by using 3.6 Mt less synthetic N; raise farmer income by 20% (84 billion yuan); raise wheat and maize yields by 32% (73.5 Mt/yr) when grown after alternative crops; and raise soil health scores by 45% (p. 5).


## 5. Conclusions

1. Diversifying wheat–maize with legumes (peanut, soybean) and cash crops (sweet potato) can increase food production, reduce net GHG emissions and improve soil health together. 2. Reduced synthetic N and higher soil carbon sequestration drive the GHG benefit. 3. The authors recommend diversified cropping as a key consideration in agricultural policy and a priority for on-farm decisions, designed for local conditions (p. 8).


## 6. Limitations

- Results come mainly from one representative research station; multi-location experiments are needed (p. 11).
- Inter-annual weather is highly variable, so the authors call multiyear sustainability assessments tentative (p. 8).
- Regional benefits are upscaled estimates, not measurements (p. 5).
- Our assessment: rice was not tested, so paddy CH₄ is outside the results; six years of SOC data may overstate long-term rates; research-station management may exceed what farmers achieve.
