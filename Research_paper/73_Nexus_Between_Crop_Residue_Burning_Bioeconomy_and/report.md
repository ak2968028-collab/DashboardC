#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Using the IPCC (1996) formula R = P × N × D × B × F, the authors estimate that India generated 516.8 Mt of dry crop residue in 2017–18 and burned 116.3 Mt, releasing about 176.1 Tg CO₂, 313.9 Gg CH₄, 8.14 Gg N₂O, 453.4 Gg PM₂.₅ and 935.9 Gg PM₁₀. Wheat, rice and sugarcane give about 80% of the burning. The burned amount rests on the IPCC default that 25% of all residue is burned. The paper proposes a four-pronged bioeconomy model to end burning.


## 1. Background

Residue burning in north-west India causes air pollution, health problems, greenhouse gas emissions and loss of soil organic matter. It continues despite the 2014 National Policy for Management of Crop Residues, partly because the rice–wheat turnaround is short.


## 2. Aim of the study

To  estimate  residue  burning  and  its  emissions  for  India,  recommend  crop  residue  management interventions, and propose a residue management–bioeconomy model.


## 3. What the authors did

Crop production for eight crop groups from 1950–51 to 2017–18 was combined with crop-specific residue ratios and dry-matter fractions, an IPCC default burned fraction of 0.25 and burn efficiency of 0.9 (Equation 1.1, Table 1). Emissions were dry matter burned × emission factor.

## 5. Conclusions

1. Residue generation is rising with intensification and mechanisation. 2. The estimates can be a proxy for a national inventory of burning pollutants. 3. A four-pronged bioeconomy model (smart farming, waste bioeconomy, capacity building, policy) can cut burning and support several SDGs.


## 6. Limitations

- One burned fraction (25%) for all crops and states, from IPCC 1996.
- No state or district breakdown despite the north-west focus.
- The bioeconomy model is conceptual, without costs.
