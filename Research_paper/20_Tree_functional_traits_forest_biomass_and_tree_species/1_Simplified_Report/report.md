#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

From 454 mature single-species stands in 136 sites, tree traits (mainly leaf photosynthetic capacity, Amax) and standing biomass explained about half (48.5%) of local variation in forest SOC. Trait effects were strongest in boreal climates and on acidic, poor, coarse soils, and weak in the warm-wet tropics. Mixed forests stored more SOC only when they also produced more biomass (r = +0.61). The authors recommend more biomass, more species and traits matched to the site, and protecting natural forests in the tropics.


## 1. Background

Forests store carbon in trees and soils in similar amounts. Tree biomass reaches limits in many regions and is vulnerable to windthrow, drought, wildfire, pests and disease, so storing carbon in forest soils is less risky (p. 2). How tree species influence soil organic carbon (SOC) was unclear and hotly debated (p. 1).


## 2. Aim of the study

To test how tree functional traits, standing biomass and species diversity interact with climate and soil to control forest SOC, and to suggest how to manage forests for more soil carbon (pp. 1–2).


## 3. What the authors did

The authors compiled 114 articles covering 454 mature single-species stands in 136 sites, comparing 178 species from 35 families growing under comparable conditions; stands were at least 30 years old and peat soils were excluded (pp. 2, 6). SOC was compared on an equivalent soil mass basis. Data on two- species mixtures and on SOC stability were extracted for 29 and 30 sites (p. 2).


## 4. Main findings

- A model based on Amax and standing biomass explained 48.5% of SOC variance (37.0% Amax, 11.5% biomass) (p. 2).
- Conservative species (low Amax, often ectomycorrhizal gymnosperms) had larger SOC pools than acquisitive species (often arbuscular-mycorrhizal angiosperms); gymnosperms stored more SOC on average (p. 3).
- Trait effects were strongest under boreal climate and on acidic, nutrient-poor, coarse-textured soils (p. 1).
- In warm-wet tropical regions, traits did not play an important role in SOC storage (p. 5).
- Mixtures increased SOC only when they overyielded in biomass; SOC gain correlated with biomass gain (r = +0.61, P = 0.016) (p. 5, Fig. 5).
- Mycorrhizal type and root traits influenced SOC decomposability (p. 4, Fig. 4).

## 5. Conclusions

The forest carbon sink can be optimised by (1) increasing standing biomass, (2) increasing tree species richness, and (3) choosing composition by tree traits suited to local conditions (p. 1). In tropical areas the most urgent goal is to protect or restore natural forests. At mid-latitudes or on fertile soils broadleaf species are suggested, and at high latitudes or on poor soils needleleaf species (p. 5).


## 6. Limitations

- Data are scarce for Africa, Asia (except China), the tropics and Russia, where selection criteria were applied flexibly (p. 6).
- Mixed-stand evidence comes from 29 sites with two-species mixtures (pp. 2, 6).
- Mostly topsoil data; deep-soil data were rarely available (p. 6).
- Results concern forest stands, not farm trees or agroforestry. CORRECTION TO THE EARLIER NOTES The paper links broadleaf species to mid-latitudes or fertile soils and needleleaf species to high latitudes or poor soils (p. 5); it does not recommend both at the same latitudes.
