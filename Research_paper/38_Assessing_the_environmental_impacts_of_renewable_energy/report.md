#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Using quarterly Chinese data for 1990–2022, the authors regress CO₂ and total GHG emissions on the shares of renewable and fossil energy in final energy use, with GDP, exports, population and coal rents as controls. A higher renewable share goes with lower emissions and a higher fossil share with higher emissions, and Granger tests show the direction runs from the energy mix to emissions, not back. China still relies on fossil fuels (79% of final energy on average). Note that the paper's 'air pollution' variable is CO₂ and GHG emissions, not air quality.


## 1. Background

Air pollution harms health and economies: 6.7 million deaths were linked to indoor and outdoor pollution in 2019, 4.3 million of them to outdoor pollution (p. 1). China, the second-largest economy, emitted about 30% of global CO₂ in 2020 and is the largest methane emitter (about 26%). Its 14th Five- Year Plan (2021–2025) uses 'dual control': a cap on energy use and a falling energy intensity of GDP (pp. 1–2).


## 2. Aim of the study

To test how renewable and non-renewable energy affect China's carbon emissions, with two hypotheses: H1, renewable use is negatively related to pollution as the economy grows; H2, stricter regulation raises renewable use, which Granger-causes lower pollution (pp. 5–6).


## 3. What the authors did

They used quarterly World Bank and National Bureau of Statistics data for 1990–2022 (128 observations, winsorised at the 1st and 99th percentiles). Dependent variables were CO₂ emissions from fossil fuels and cement (kt) and total GHG emissions (kt CO₂e). Independent variables were the renewable share and the non-renewable share of final energy consumption. Controls were coal rents, GDP, exports and population. ARIMA regressions gave baseline effects, and Granger causality tests with two lags tested the direction both ways (pp. 6–7). CORRECTION TO THE OLD SUMMARY ARIMA is used here as a regression with controls, not for forecasting, and 'air pollution' is measured only as CO₂ and GHG emissions (p. 6).

### 4.2 Granger causality

Lagged  renewable  and  fossil  shares  significantly  predicted  CO₂  and  GHG  emissions,  while  lagged emissions did not predict either energy share. The authors conclude that policy-driven renewables have reduced emissions, but rising pollution has not pushed policymakers to speed up renewables (Tables 4– 5, pp. 8–10).


### 4.3 Energy mix

Mean shares over the sample were 79.23% non-renewable and 18.79% renewable (p. 8). The renewable share rose from about 10% in 1990 to nearly 30% in 2021 (p. 11).


### 4.4 Points to read with care

- Coefficients are described 'per kiloton', but the energy variables are percentage shares (p. 8).
- The text gives −0.00529 for the renewable → CO₂ lag total, Table 4 gives −0.00629; for GHG the text says renewables 'increase' emissions by 0.00201, while Table 5 shows −0.00201 (pp. 8–10).
- The paper says China aims for 'carbon dioxide neutrality by 2030'; the 2020 pledge was a CO₂ peak before 2030 and neutrality before 2060 (p. 2).

## 5. Conclusions

1. Renewable energy use reduces, and fossil energy use increases, China's CO₂ and GHG emissions, with one-way causality.

2. China still relies heavily on fossil fuels, so the shift to cleaner energy must continue. 3. Results are consistent with the Environmental Kuznets Curve and the Porter Hypothesis. 4. Developed nations should help cut renewable costs and support developing countries (pp. 10–11).


## 6. Limitations

- China only, at national level; geography is not considered (p. 11).
- Statistical (Granger) causality, not physical causation; energy shares and emissions are highly correlated with GDP and exports (Table 2).
- No direct air-quality pollutant is modelled.
- Not applicable to rural or village scale without new data.
