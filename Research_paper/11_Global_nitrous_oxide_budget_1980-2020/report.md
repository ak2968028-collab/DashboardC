## 1. Background

N₂O is a long-lived greenhouse gas and ozone-depleting substance. It rose by nearly 25%, from 270 ppb in 1750 to 336 ppb in 2022, higher than at any time in the last 800,000 years. Growth exceeded 1.3 ppb/yr in 2020 and 2021, the fastest since 1980. IPCC AR6 attributes 6.4% of enhanced GHG radiative forcing over 1750–2022 to N₂O (pp. 2544–2545).


## 2. Aim of the study

To quantify global N₂O sources and sinks for 1980–2020 in 21 natural and human categories and 18 regions, using bottom-up and top-down methods, and to identify trends and uncertainties (p. 2545).


## 3. What the authors did

Bottom-up (BU) estimates combined inventories (EDGAR v7.0, FAOSTAT), statistical extrapolation of flux measurements and process-based land and ocean models. Top-down (TD) estimates used atmospheric measurements in inverse models. Human sources were split into direct agricultural (fertiliser and manure applied to soils, manure left on pasture, manure management, aquaculture), other direct (fossil fuel and industry, waste and wastewater, biomass burning) and indirect emissions from nitrogen deposited or leached (pp. 2545, 2564). UNITS Results are in Tg N per year (million tonnes of N in N₂O). Multiply by 44/28 to get N₂O mass, then by its GWP for CO₂e.

### 4.2 Regions

- Europe, the top emitter in the 1980s (23.6% of human N₂O), cut emissions by 31%, mostly from fossil fuel and industry (pp. 2546, 2574).
- China (+82%) and South Asia (+92%) grew most; in the 2010s they gave 17.8% and 13.2% of human N₂O and 40% and 30% of the global increase (p. 2574).
- In South Asia, direct agricultural N₂O rose from 0.19 to 0.55 Tg N/yr (1980–2020) because of more N fertiliser (p. 2572). 4.3 Atmosphere and uncertainty The atmospheric burden rose from 1527 Tg N (2000s) to 1606 Tg N (2020). Satellite data suggest the lifetime fell from 119 to 117 years. Inversions are least certain over South America, Africa and central and southern Asia; soil N₂O is least certain in tropical forests and in high-fertiliser regions including northern India, eastern China and the US Corn Belt (pp. 2546, 2563).

## 5. Conclusions

1. Human N₂O emissions, driven mainly by agriculture, keep rising and concentrations exceed all CMIP6 scenarios (p. 2545). 2. Tier 2/3 national methods are needed where nitrogen surplus is high, such as eastern China, India and the USA (p. 2576). 3. The authors propose a global N₂O monitoring and modelling network, from surface to stratosphere, to support the UNFCCC Global Stocktake (pp. 2545–2546).


## 6. Limitations

- BU ranges are wide (2020: 10.6–27.0 Tg N/yr) (p. 2545).
- Inversions are poorly constrained in southern Asia because stations are few (p. 2546).
- Results are regional, not district or field level.
- Effects of rising CO₂, forest conversion and biomass burning are poorly quantified (p. 2546).
