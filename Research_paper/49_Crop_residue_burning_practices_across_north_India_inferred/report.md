#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

MODIS misses many small, short crop fires in north India, and global inventories differ ten-fold there. The authors surveyed over 2,000 farmers in Punjab, Haryana, UP and Bihar and used the answers to correct satellite fire power for small fires, haze, partial burning and the daily fire cycle. Post-monsoon burning averaged 10.4 Tg dry matter and 14.9 Tg CO₂ a year (2003–2018), 3.4 times global inventories for OC+BC. Punjab dominates, but UP and Bihar still harvest mostly by hand, so burning could rise 67–207% as combines spread.


## 1. Background

Post-monsoon (October–November) crop burning worsens air quality across north India. MODIS has 500 m–1 km pixels and two overpasses a day per satellite, so it misses small, short fires and fires under haze; inventories based on it differ by up to an order of magnitude (p. 1). Burning spread with combine harvesters from the mid-to-late 1980s (p. 2).


## 2. Aim of the study

To use household survey data to bridge gaps in satellite observations and improve post-monsoon fire emission estimates for Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar over 2003–2018, producing a gridded inventory (SAGE-IGP) (pp. 1–2).


## 3. What the authors did

In 2017, over 2,000 farmers were asked about the 2016–17 rice harvest and residue burning; 20 households per village were chosen by stratified purposive sampling to represent landholding sizes and

social classes. In 2018, the survey was repeated with 90% of the same participants for 2017–18, adding questions on burning start year, method, time, wait and reasons (p. 3). MODIS FRP was adjusted for VIIRS small fires, cloud/haze gaps, partial burning and the diurnal cycle, converted to dry matter (0.41 kg/MJ) and emissions, and validated bottom-up for Punjab (pp. 7–8, 12). BOTTOM-UP FORMULA (EQ. 4, P. 7) Emissions = fraction burned × crop production × residue-to-crop ratio (1.4–1.8) × dry-matter fraction (0.82–0.88) × combustion completeness (0.89 whole-field, 0.67 partial) × emission factor.


## 4. Main findings

The corrections raise FRP by 109% (VIIRS), 142% (haze gap-fill), 33% (partial burning) and 361% (diurnal cycle). Average seasonal burning is 10.4 ± 3 Tg dry matter, giving 65 ± 18 Gg OC, 5.6 ± 1.6 Gg BC, 791 ± 225 Gg CO and 14.9 ± 4.2 Tg CO₂. Punjab gives 70% of dry matter and 67% of aerosols. ## 5. Conclusions

1. Household surveys and crop statistics can constrain fire timing and fuel consumption, the main sources of disagreement among inventories. 2. SAGE-IGP gives daily 0.25° emissions for 2003–2018 for air-quality and health studies. 3. Burning is likely to expand among smallholders in the eastern plain, where satellites see fires poorly (pp. 16–17).


## 6. Limitations

- Survey samples for Haryana, UP and Bihar are only 10–29% of Punjab's.
- Burn rates may be under-reported because of fire bans; daily cycle and partial-burn share assumed uniform per state and over time.
- Post-monsoon rice residue only; state budgets disaggregated to a grid with statistical noise.
