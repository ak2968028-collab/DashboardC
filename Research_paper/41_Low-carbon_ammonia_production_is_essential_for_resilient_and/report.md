#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Synthetic nitrogen fertiliser feeds about half the world but is made from fossil fuels. Ammonia production emitted 450–500 Mt CO₂e in 2020 and, with field N₂O and transport, the fertiliser chain emits 1.1–1.3 Gt CO₂e a year. Supply is concentrated (China 28%, USA 9%, India 8% of capacity), and 1.8 billion people depend on imported fertiliser or imported gas. Carbon capture can cut production emissions by about 70% but keeps gas dependence; electrolytic and biomass routes cut more but cost 2–3 times as much and need 100–300 times more land and water. Small decentralised plants could help the global south, with policy support.


## 1. Background

Nitrogen comes from manure (33%) and synthetic fertiliser (67%). From 2000 to 2021 the world population grew 25% and N-fertiliser use 30%, and demand is forecast to rise 2.8–3.7% a year. In 2020, 183 Mt NH₃ was produced, 70% for agriculture (p. 610). In 2021, 46% of nitrogen used worldwide (49 Mt N, US$39 billion) was traded (p. 611).


## 2. Aim of the study

To map the global N-fertiliser supply chain and its vulnerabilities, and to compare the trade-offs of low- carbon ammonia pathways, centralised and decentralised.


## 3. What the authors did

They built a dataset of demand, 407 ammonia plants in 61 countries and 2021 trade flows; calculated a supply-chain vulnerability index (1 minus domestic capacity over agricultural use, with and without gas imports) and the number of people fed by N fertiliser; and compared five pathways (BAU gas, gas + CCS, renewable electrolysis, biomass + CCS, decentralised electrolysis) on cost, GHG, water, land, electricity, fossil dependence and supply-chain complexity (pp. 611–615).


## 4. Main findings


### 4.1 Emissions and energy

Ammonia production alone emitted 450–500 Mt CO₂e (1.3% of the global total) and used about 2% of final energy (8.6 EJ) in 2020; use-phase emissions (~660 Mt CO₂e) and transport (~30 Mt) bring the total to 1,100–1,300 Mt CO₂e a year. A cradle-to-grave view gives 7,400 Mt CO₂e, 41% of food-system emissions (pp. 610–611). Plants use 7.7–10.1 kWh per kg NH₃; 99% of the hydrogen is fossil-based (70% gas, 26% coal). Direct emissions average 2.6–2.9 t CO₂ per t NH₃ (3.2–4.5 t from coal) (pp. 611–612).


### 4.2 Supply, demand and vulnerability

China holds 28% of capacity, the USA 9% and India 8% (45% together). In 2021 China used 20% of N fertiliser,  India 18% and the USA 11%; India is a net importer. Of 3.9 billion people who rely on N fertiliser, 1.2 billion depend on imports, rising to  1.8 billion with imported gas. European ammonia capacity fell 32% in 2022 after the invasion of Ukraine (p. 612).

### 4.4 Decentralised production

Small plants near farms can reduce transport costs, emissions, import reliance and price volatility, and need less capital; they may become competitive in remote, import-reliant regions by about 2030. But only 3% of N is applied directly as ammonia, so on-farm handling or conversion adds cost, and the effect on field nitrogen use is uncertain (pp. 616–617).


## 5. Conclusions

1. Decarbonising ammonia production is needed for both climate goals and food security. 2. CCS cuts emissions but keeps gas dependence; electrolytic and biochemical routes cut more but cost more and need more land and water. 3. Decentralised production could raise productivity in the global south but requires policy support. 4. Interdisciplinary research must weigh costs, N₂O, water, energy and land (p. 617).


## 6. Limitations

- Focus is on production; farm-level use and N₂O are not modelled (p. 611).
- Mainly production costs; transport distances and routes are not modelled (Methods, Caveats).
- Global estimates; no farm-level or Indian district values.
