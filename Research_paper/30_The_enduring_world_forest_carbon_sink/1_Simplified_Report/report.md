#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Using forest inventory data for about 95% of the world's forests, the authors find that forests took up a steady 3.6 Pg C per year from 1990 to 2019 (3.5 in the 2010s), equal to almost half of fossil fuel emissions. The total hides big shifts: temperate and tropical regrowth sinks rose by about 30%, while boreal and intact tropical sinks fell by a third. Tropical deforestation (2.2 Pg C per year) cancelled about two-thirds of the benefit. Stopping deforestation is the most important way to keep the sink.


## 1. Background

Carbon dioxide uptake by land ecosystems is critical for moderating climate change, and forests are its main part. Atmospheric CO₂ passed 420 ppm in 2023. Reaching net zero by 2050 will need large-scale negative emissions of up to 6 Pg C per year, and land has historically lost 180 Pg C to land-use change, part of which can be restored (ms pp. 1–2). Remote sensing and models can map land sinks at high resolution but struggle to give long-term baselines  and  often  disagree.  Forest  inventories,  based  on  tree-by-tree  measurements,  give  an independent, ground-based view.


## 2. Aim of the study

To give a ground-based, long-term assessment of how much carbon the world's forests took up over three decades (1990–2019), where it is stored, and how the sink changed by biome and carbon pool.


## 3. What the authors did

- Synthesised forest inventory and long-term monitoring data for boreal, temperate and tropical biomes, 1990–2019, combined with remote-sensing-based forest areas.
- Covered about 4.0 billion ha, 95% of global forest; remote unmanaged northern forests, some sparse Asian forests, mangroves and non-forest peatlands were not included (ms p. 21).
- Estimated stocks and stock changes in five pools: living biomass, dead wood, litter, soil and harvested wood products.
- Split boreal and temperate forests into remaining and new (afforested or reforested) forests, and tropical forests into intact and regrowth forests.
- Subtracted tropical deforestation emissions to get the net forest sink, and compared the results with the Global Carbon Budget, DGVMs and inversions.
- Reported ± values as 95% confidence ranges (ms p. 27).

## 4. Main findings


### 4.1 Forest area and carbon stock

World forest area fell by 5% from 1990 to 2020, from 4,022 to 3,812 Mha. Tropical forests lost 273 Mha (13%), including 467 Mha (26%) of intact forest, while regrowth forests expanded by 194 Mha (56%). Temperate forests gained 52 Mha (7%) and boreal forests were stable (+12 Mha). The 2020 forest carbon stock was 870 ± 61 Pg C: 43% in living biomass, 45% in soil, 8% in dead wood and 4% in litter (ms p. 3).

### 4.3 Necromass and wood products

Necromass (dead wood, litter and soil) holds 58% of forest carbon, from 45% in tropical to 80% in boreal forests. Only about 10% of the carbon in harvested timber is stored in wood products, which supply just 6% of the global sink (0.21 Pg C per year in the 2010s) (ms pp. 8–9).


### 4.4 Comparison with fossil emissions and other estimates

Forests took up 107 Pg C since 1990, equal to 46% of fossil fuel emissions (7.8 ± 0.4 Pg C per year), but 63% of this was negated by tropical deforestation. The net forest sink (1.3 Pg C per year) is only 17% of fossil emissions but 91% of the land net sink. Because the land gross sink grew while the forest sink did not, the forest share of the land gross sink fell from 75% to 65%, and models that show rising forest uptake may overestimate future uptake (ms pp. 12, 15).


## 5. Conclusions

1. The global forest sink has endured, undiminished, for three decades, despite large regional changes. 2. It may weaken because of ageing forests, continuing deforestation and more intense disturbance. 3. The single most important action is to stop deforestation and degradation and protect old carbon stocks. 4. Reforestation, afforestation, climate-smart management and long-lived wood products can add to the sink. 5. The forest sequestration rate (~3.56 Pg C, ~13 Gt CO₂-eq per year) is a baseline for IPCC's estimate that land sectors could mitigate an extra 8–14 Gt CO₂-eq per year in 2020–2050.


## 6. Limitations

- Data quality varies across countries; uncertainty is largest in the tropics (±27%) and boreal forests (±13%).
- Soils, dead wood and forest area estimates are the least certain parts.
- Results are global and biome-level; there are no India-specific values.
- Non-forest peatlands, wetlands and mangroves are excluded.
