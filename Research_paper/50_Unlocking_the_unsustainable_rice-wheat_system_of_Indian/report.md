#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Crop residue burning in Punjab persists despite bans, machines and supposedly profitable alternatives. The authors review black carbon inventories (27–83 Gg/yr for India; 1.5–47.2 Gg/yr for Punjab), map the feedbacks driving burning in a causal loop diagram, and trace who invests in rice and wheat value chains. Price supports, procurement and a short gap between crops lock the system into burning. The Government of India has the greatest financial leverage and risk. Change needs crop diversification, circular business models and green finance, not more pressure on farmers.


## 1. Background

Black carbon from residue burning harms health and climate and can threaten farming. Punjab covers 1.5% of India's area but supplied about 27% of rice and 37% of wheat to the national stock in 2015–16; rice-wheat covers at least 80% of its gross cropped area. Minimum support prices and Food Corporation of India procurement, which reduced hunger, now sustain an unsustainable system (pp. 1–3).


## 2. Aim of the study

(a) Understand system feedbacks driving farm practices; (b) identify system-wide effects of alternatives to burning; (c) identify companies and financial actors investing in Punjab's agriculture (p. 1).


## 3. What the authors did

They compared BC estimates from five inventories, built a causal loop diagram of 37 elements and 76 interactions, mapped rice, basmati and wheat value chains, and used Prowess and Orbis to identify 166 listed companies and 1,776 shareholders (pp. 4–5, 7).

Reinforcing loops (agricultural policy; pressure to burn from groundwater rules and short rotation) and only degradation-type stabilising loops show a lock-in (p. 5). Basmati, grown mainly in districts bordering Pakistan, is rarely burned, while non-basmati covers 80% of the rice area (pp. 7, 9). The Government of India holds shares in 15 of the 25 companies owned by the top shareholders and is the single largest investor; Blackrock and Vanguard have blockholding power. Corporate actors have little risk exposure and so little incentive, but enough stake to influence change (pp. 7, 10).


## 5. Conclusions

1. The problem is not farmers but the rules, constraints and needs they respond to; solutions must enable them. 2. Do less harm (address root causes, not quick fixes) and do good (diversification, circular business models, green finance). 3. Banks and the stock exchange can set sustainability conditions and disclosure rules (pp. 10–11).


## 6. Limitations

- Punjab only; more mechanised with larger farms than eastern UP.
- BC inventories vary widely; causal loop diagrams are qualitative and omit adaptation.
- Investor analysis covers listed companies only; millers are mostly private.
