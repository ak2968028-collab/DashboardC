#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Peatlands, mangroves, salt marshes and seagrass meadows cover about 1% of Earth's surface but store 20% of ecosystem organic carbon, because vegetation and landforms reinforce each other (biogeomorphic feedbacks). These wetlands are lost at about 1% a year, releasing 0.5 Pg C a year, 5% of human carbon emissions, and degraded wetlands keep emitting for centuries. Conservation comes first; restoration works best when it restarts the feedbacks, for example by planting in clumps or rewetting peat at landscape scale.


## 1. Background

Burial of organic matter is the largest carbon-sequestering process on the planet. Wetlands cover just 2% of Earth's surface but store more than 20% of global organic ecosystem carbon, and their sequestration rates can be orders of magnitude higher than those of land and ocean ecosystems (p. 8). Restoring this function requires understanding why.


## 2. Aim of the study

To compare carbon stocks and sequestration in oceans, forests and wetlands; explain how vegetation– landform feedbacks make wetlands carbon hotspots; show how human disruption turns them into sources; and show how restoration can restart the feedbacks.


## 3. What the authors did

They compiled published estimates of carbon stocks, densities and recent sequestration rates (Fig. 1, Table S1), global extent and loss rates (Table 1) and carbon released after land-use change (Fig. 3, Table

S2), focusing on 'biogeomorphic wetlands': peatlands, where vegetation retains water and builds peat, and coastal wetlands, where vegetation traps sediment (p. 8).

### 4.2 Biogeomorphic feedbacks

In 45% of all wetlands, biogeomorphic feedbacks shape landscapes (p. 9). They are either productivity- stimulating (fens and coastal wetlands: more resources, more growth) or decomposition-limiting (bogs: waterlogged, acidic, nutrient-poor conditions slow decay). Salt marsh sediment can accrete up to 25 mm/yr, mangroves 21 mm/yr and seagrass 10 mm/yr (p. 12).


### 4.3 Losses: from sink to source

Wetlands decline at 0.4–3.3% a year (boreal peatlands stable). Salt marshes have lost 42%, mangroves 35% and seagrass 29% of their area; temperate and tropical peatlands are 57% and 41% degraded. Average loss of about 1% a year releases 0.5 Pg C a year, 5% of human emissions. Drained peat loses soil carbon for a century; coastal soils erode and oxidise quickly (pp. 12–13, Table 1).


### 4.4 Restoration

Restoration often fails (< 50% success) and costs US$750–1,000,000 per ha, because feedbacks only work above a minimum patch size and density. Clumped planting doubled yields; biodegradable structures can mimic large patches; landscape-scale rewetting and paludiculture restore peatlands; water tables can be set to balance CO₂ and CH₄ (pp. 14–15).


## 5. Conclusions

1. Biogeomorphic wetlands are the world's biotic carbon hotspots. 2. Stopping their loss through conservation is of utmost importance. 3. Restoration that recovers landscape-forming feedbacks can turn them from sources back into sinks, supporting the Paris Agreement and the UN Decade on Ecosystem Restoration (p. 15).


## 6. Limitations

- Global literature compilation; loss and emission estimates rest on assumed emission factors (Fig. 3).
- Covers peatlands and coastal wetlands only; inland river floodplains and ponds are not assessed.
- The net climate effect of permafrost thaw is unknown, and rewetting can release CH₄ (pp. 13, 15).
