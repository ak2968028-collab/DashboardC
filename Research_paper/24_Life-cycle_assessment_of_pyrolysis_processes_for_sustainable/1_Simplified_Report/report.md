#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

This review compares life cycle assessments (LCAs) of biochar made from agro-residues. Results are hard to compare because studies use different functional units and boundaries, but the net carbon benefit is promising. For rice straw, one cited LCA found savings of 1.10–1.64 t CO₂-eq per tonne depending on pyrolysis type. Soil use beat energy use in one comparison, wet feedstocks gave little benefit, and village brick kilns saved less than stoves or large plants. The authors call for locally designed pyrolysis and for linking LCA with circular-economy and cost analysis.


## 1. Background

Agro-residues are primary field wastes (rice, wheat and cotton straw) or secondary processing wastes (rice husks, peanut shells, cobs) (AM p. 6). In many regions they are abandoned or burned, releasing CO₂, smog and particulate matter (AM p. 3). Pyrolysis at 300 °C and above converts them into biochar, bio-oil and syngas, and LCA tests whether this gives a real net carbon benefit (AM pp. 3, 8).


## 2. Aim of the study

To critically analyse recent LCAs of biochar production from different agro-residues, compare pyrolysis technologies, and assess biochar's net carbon benefit as an energy source, soil amendment or activated- carbon substitute (AM p. 2).


## 3. What the authors did

The authors described residue properties, pre-treatment, pyrolysis routes and reactor types (AM pp. 6– 10), explained LCA goal and scope, functional units, system boundaries and inventories (AM pp. 11–20),

and compared impact results and sensitivity analyses from published LCAs summarised in Tables 2–3 (AM pp. 20–25, 45–48).


## 4. Main findings

- Righi et al. found −386 to −933 kg CO₂-eq per t feedstock for soil amendment versus −240 to −787 for energy supply (AM p. 21).
- For rice straw, Gong et al. found reductions of 1.14, 1.64 and 1.10 t CO₂-eq per t with fast, intermediate and slow pyrolysis; for corn stover 1.75, 1.12 and 1.84 (AM pp. 22–23).
- Crop residues pyrolysed at 400–700 °C avoided 200–470 kg CO₂-eq per t (AM p. 22); Clare et al. reported −1.06 t CO₂-eq per t, and Dai et al. a potential of 1.41 million t CO₂-eq for Chinese crop residues (AM p. 21).
- Wet orange peel gave only −5.5 kg CO₂-eq per t because of drying energy (AM p. 22).
- For rice husk, a brick kiln gave −229, a stove −318 and a large plant −360 kg CO₂-eq per t (AM p. 24).
- Extending the boundary to soil application makes CO₂-eq sequestration achievable (AM p. 21).

## 5. Conclusions

Available studies show good carbon reduction potential for biochar as an energy source, soil amendment and activated-carbon substitute, and pyrolysis design should be customised to the local context (AM p. 27). Biochar is not a silver bullet; co-products, economics and circular-economy modelling should be included in future LCAs (AM pp. 26–27).


## 6. Limitations

- Numbers are ranges from studies with different functional units, boundaries and assumptions (AM p. 2).
- Cases come from China, Southeast Asia, Europe, Australia and the Americas; none from India (Tables 2–3).
- Manpower is usually left out of the system boundary, which favours labour-intensive small reactors (AM p. 23).
