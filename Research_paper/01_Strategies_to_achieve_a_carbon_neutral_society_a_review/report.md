#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Only 4.5% of 198 countries have reached carbon neutrality, and most others aim for dates after 2050. The review sets out how to get there: cut emissions with policy and sector technologies, remove CO₂ with negative emission technologies and nature-based solutions, map emissions to target effort, and check every option with life cycle analysis.


## 1. Background

Industrial growth and heavy use of fossil fuels have released large amounts of greenhouse gases. The paper reports that atmospheric CO₂ rose from about 285 ppm around 1850 to about 419 ppm in 2022. Over 1850–2022 the UK Met Office estimates a rise in global average surface temperature of 0.97 to 1.21 °C, with a central estimate of 1.09 °C. Global GHG emissions are expected to rise by 50% by 2050, mainly from CO₂ from non-renewable energy. Under the Paris Agreement, adopted on 12 December 2015 by 197 parties, countries agreed to keep warming below 2 °C and to work to limit it to 1.5 °C. By February 2021, 124 countries had declared that they intend to become carbon neutral by 2050 or 2060.


> **DEFINITION:**

Carbon neutrality (net-zero carbon emissions) is reached when the CO₂ or greenhouse gases that a country, company, product, activity or person emits, directly or indirectly, over a period are balanced by carbon offsets or removals. The authors stress that emission cuts alone are not enough; CO₂ must also be removed from the atmosphere, for example by sequestration in land and ocean ecosystems.


## 2. Aim of the study

The review presents a strategy to reach a carbon neutral society. It:

- examines the outcomes of COP26 (Glasgow, 31 October–12 November 2021);
- compiles carbon neutrality initiatives and target years for 198 countries;
- reviews the links and synergies between adaptation and mitigation strategies;
- reviews methods for mapping direct and indirect carbon emissions;
- summarises policies and technologies that cut emissions and that remove CO₂;
- reviews life cycle analysis (LCA) of carbon neutral systems and the environmental, social and economic impacts of carbon neutrality.

## 3. What the authors did

This is a literature review, not a field or laboratory study. The authors collected published studies, policy documents and national pledges. They compiled three tables: national carbon neutrality initiatives (Table 1), methods used to map direct and indirect emissions (Table 2), and LCA studies of carbon neutral systems (Table 3). They group solutions into two routes: (1) reducing emissions through policy (carbon trading, carbon tax) and sector technologies in energy, industry, buildings and cities, transport, and agriculture, food and waste; and (2)  removing CO₂ from the atmosphere with negative emission technologies (NETs) and nature-based solutions.

### 4.2 COP26

COP26 made progress in four areas, summed up as coal, cars, cash and trees: phasing out coal, moving to electric transport, delivering the US$100 billion a year that developed countries pledged in 2010 to developing countries, and protecting forests and habitats. Before the summit, enhanced 2030 targets had been submitted only by countries responsible for 55% of global GHG emissions, and long-term strategies by countries responsible for 32%.


### 4.3 Adaptation and mitigation

Some measures help both mitigation and adaptation, such as distributed solar power, afforestation, agroforestry and green roofs. Others involve trade-offs: hydropower cuts emissions but competes for

local  water.  Synergies  remain  weak  in  practice:  of  885 European  climate  action  plans,  only  147 considered both mitigation and adaptation, and only about a quarter analysed their synergies in depth. Cameroon's policies focused on mitigation only.

### 4.5 Reducing emissions

- Policy: a study of China's carbon trading (2008–2018) found that emissions fell sharply in several provinces after trading began. A carbon-tax incentive study for aviation found that, under the right conditions, incentives could push airlines to raise fuel efficiency.
- Energy: moving from fossil fuels to renewable energy is described as the most important approach in the energy sector. European models show carbon neutrality by mid-century is technically feasible.
- Industry: in UK iron and steel, heat recovery and similar technologies could cut energy use by 18% and GHG emissions by 12%.
- Buildings and cities: resilient design, decentralised energy, better building envelopes and timber construction (1 m³ of wood stores about half a tonne of carbon).
- Transport: electrification was found to be the best way to cut transport emissions; electricity could supply 72.3% of EU transport energy with existing technology. Biogas upgraded to biomethane could reach 30 billion m³/yr in EU CNG and LNG vehicles by 2030.

### 4.7 Removing CO₂

The NETs reviewed include bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS), direct air capture, biochar,  soil  carbon  sequestration,  afforestation  and  reforestation,  enhanced  weathering,  wetland construction and restoration, ocean alkalinity enhancement and ocean fertilisation. In a Scottish study (Alcalde et al. 2018), enhanced weathering had the highest costs (US$25–1600 per t CO₂), BECCS and forestation were less expensive, and biochar and soil carbon sequestration could be cost-effective. A mix of BECCS, soil carbon sequestration and enhanced weathering could reduce emissions by 8.3–36.8 Mt CO₂; at maximum capacity, the mix could remove up to 89.8% of Scotland's annual emissions. Direct air capture could deliver 3 Gt CO₂/yr of negative emissions by 2035.


### 4.8 Life cycle analysis and impacts

Every decarbonisation technique and carbon removal project should pass a life cycle assessment to avoid greenwashing; the authors call for cradle-to-grave analysis of whole systems. Carbon neutrality is expected to improve air quality and restore ecosystems, create jobs in clean energy, and cost jobs in coal and related sectors.


## 5. Conclusions

1. The first step is to shift from fossil fuels to renewable energy and to develop low-carbon technologies. The energy transition should electrify energy services, raise energy efficiency and promote renewables. 2. Low-carbon agriculture, changes in diet (more vegetarian food, less meat, less food waste) and adding value to food and farm waste are essential steps toward net zero. 3. Buildings should be resilient to natural hazards; cities need decentralised energy systems and technologies such as electric vehicles, the Internet of things and big data. 4. Emission mapping tools help decision-makers target the places and sectors that matter most. 5. Negative emission technologies are needed alongside emission cuts, and every option should be checked by life cycle analysis.


## 6. Limitations

- It is a broad global review. Most case studies come from China and Europe; it gives no village-level or Indian emission factors (India appears only as a 2070 target in Table 1 and in a cross-country study).
- Nearly all numbers are quoted from other studies, not newly measured. Check the original source before citing a value.
- Our assessment: agriculture gets a short section compared with energy, industry and transport.
