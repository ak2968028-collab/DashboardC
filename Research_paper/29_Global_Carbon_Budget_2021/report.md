#### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

In 2020, humans emitted 10.2 ± 0.8 GtC of CO₂ (37.4 GtCO₂), even though fossil emissions fell 5.4% in the COVID year; preliminary data show a +4.8% rebound in 2021. About half of the emissions stay in the atmosphere, with the rest taken up roughly equally by the ocean and land. From January 2022, the remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C (50% likelihood) is 120 GtC, about 11 years of 2021 emissions.


## 1. Background

Knowing how much CO₂ humans emit, and where it goes (atmosphere, ocean or land), is critical for understanding the carbon cycle, setting climate policy and projecting future climate change. Atmospheric CO₂ rose from about 277 ppm in 1750 to 412.4 ± 0.1 ppm in 2020 (p. 1920). The Global Carbon Budget is an annual update by the Global Carbon Project. The 2021 edition gives final data for 2020, preliminary data for 2021 and an updated remaining carbon budget. THE BUDGET EQUATION EFOS + ELUC = GATM + SOCEAN + SLAND + BIM. Emissions from fossil fuels and land-use change equal the growth in atmospheric CO₂ plus the ocean and land sinks; the budget imbalance (BIM) is what is left over and measures imperfect data and understanding.


## 2. Aim of the study

To describe and synthesise the datasets and methods that quantify the five major components of the global carbon budget and their uncertainties, and to document changes in methods and data since the previous editions.


## 3. What the authors did

- EFOS: energy statistics and cement production data, with the cement carbonation sink subtracted.
- ELUC: land-use data and three bookkeeping models (H&N2017, BLUE, OSCAR); 17 DGVMs as an independent estimate.
- GATM: direct measurements of atmospheric CO₂ (1 ppm = 2.124 GtC).
- SOCEAN: eight global ocean biogeochemistry models and seven observation-based fCO₂ data products.
- SLAND: 17 dynamic global vegetation models.
- Checks: six atmospheric inversions, atmospheric O₂ data and Earth system models.
- New in this edition: the first approach to reconcile the ELUC estimate with national greenhouse gas inventories (NGHGIs). All uncertainties are reported as ±1σ. Values are in GtC; multiply by 3.664 to get GtCO₂.

### 4.2 The last decade (2011–2020)

Fossil emissions averaged 9.5 ± 0.5 GtC/yr (90% of total emissions) and land-use change 1.1 ± 0.7 GtC/yr (10%). Of the total of 10.6 ± 0.8 GtC/yr, the atmosphere kept 47% (5.1 GtC/yr), the ocean took 26% (2.8 GtC/yr) and the land 29% (3.1 GtC/yr), with an imbalance of about 3% (pp. 1920, 1949–1950). Fossil emission growth slowed to 0.6% per year, from 3.0% per year in the 2000s (p. 1932).


### 4.3 Countries

Over the last 10 years, China's fossil emissions rose +1.0% per year (dominating the global trend) and India's +3.9% per year, while the EU27 fell −1.9% and the USA −1.1% per year (p. 1932). Over 1850–2020 the USA emitted the most, 110 GtC (25%), followed by the EU (80 GtC, 18%) and China (60 GtC, 14%). Coal supplied 46% of cumulative fossil CO₂, oil 35% and gas 14%.


### 4.4 The 2021 projection

Fossil CO₂ rebounded by +4.8% (4.2% to 5.4%) to about 9.9 GtC (36.4 GtCO₂), close to the 2019 level. Compared with 2019, emissions were 5.7% higher in China (3.0 GtC) and 3.2% higher in India (0.74 GtC), but 4.5% lower in the USA, 5.3% lower in the EU and 4.0% lower in the rest of the world (p. 1920). Atmospheric CO₂ was set to reach 414.7 ppm, 50% above pre-industrial levels.


### 4.5 Land-use change

Net ELUC is the small difference between near-constant gross emissions (3.8 ± 0.6 GtC/yr in 2011–2020) and growing gross removals (2.7 ± 0.4 GtC/yr). Cumulative ELUC for 1850–2020 was 200 ± 65 GtC, with model estimates from 140 to 270 GtC (pp. 1920, 1936). Accounting for the part of the natural sink that national inventories assign to managed land makes the two approaches consistent.


### 4.6 Cumulative emissions and remaining budget

Emissions in 1850–2020 totalled 660 ± 65 GtC, partitioned among the atmosphere (41%), ocean (26%) and land (30%), with a 25 GtC (4%) imbalance (p. 1949).

## 5. Conclusions

1. The mean and trend of the budget are consistently estimated over 1959–2020; the mean imbalance since 1960 is only 0.03 GtC/yr. 2. Discrepancies of up to about 1 GtC/yr remain in annual to semi-decadal variability. 3. Three main uncertainties: land-use change emissions; the land CO₂ flux in the northern extra-tropics; and the strength of the ocean sink over the last decade. 4. Human CO₂ emissions remain near record levels, and the 1.5 °C budget is only about 11 years of current emissions.


## 6. Limitations

- Global and national data only; no district or village values.
- Covers CO₂ only; CH₄ and N₂O are covered by other budgets (papers 2 and 11).
- Land-use change emissions are highly uncertain, and 2021 values are preliminary projections.
- Each edition revises earlier years, so cite the latest edition for current numbers (papers 26–28 are newer).
